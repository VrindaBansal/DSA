<Intuition>
Data interpretation questions come in sets of two or three that share one table or chart. The math is
almost never harder than **percents and averages** — you already know it. What makes these questions
hard is **reading the display wrong**: missing that the numbers are "in thousands," using a percent
when the question wants a count, or grabbing the wrong year.

So the skill here is mostly a habit: **spend the first 15 seconds understanding the display** before
you look at any question. Then do simple math, and **estimate** whenever the answer choices let you.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Read the display first

Before answering anything, check these four things:

1. **Title and units.** "Sales (in thousands of dollars)" means 450 is really $450,000.
2. **What the numbers are.** Counts? Percents of a total? Percents of *what* total?
3. **The scale.** What does each gridline stand for? Does the axis start at 0?
4. **Footnotes and legends.** Which bar or line is which? Any note like "totals may not add to 100%
   due to rounding"?
</Concept>

<Example title="Watch the units">
<DataChart c={{
  type: 'bar',
  title: 'Museum Visitors by Month (thousands)',
  categories: ['Jan', 'Feb', 'Mar', 'Apr'],
  series: [{ name: 'Visitors', values: [12.5, 9.8, 15.2, 18.0] }],
  yLabel: 'Visitors (thousands)',
  yMax: 20,
  yStep: 5,
  showValues: true,
}} />

How many more people visited the museum in April than in February?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Read the units.** The title says **thousands**, so April is 18,000 visitors and February is
9,800.

**Step 2 — Subtract.** 18,000 − 9,800 = **8,200** more visitors.

Answering "8.2" is the trap — that's 8.2 *thousand*.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Percent change vs. actual change

Two different questions that sound alike:

- **"By how much did it increase?"** → the **actual** change: new − old.
- **"By what percent did it increase?"** → the **percent** change:

$$
\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%
$$

The same actual increase is a **bigger percent** when the starting number is **smaller**. Going from
50 to 80 (+30) is a 60% increase, but going from 400 to 480 (+80, a bigger jump) is only 20%.

**On a line graph,** the steepest segment has the biggest **actual** change. That's not always the
biggest **percent** change — check the starting values.
</Concept>

<Example title="Biggest percent increase">
| Region | 2020 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| North | 200 | 260 |
| South | 50 | 80 |
| West | 400 | 480 |

Which region had the greatest **percent** increase from 2020 to 2022?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Actual change for each.** North +60, South +30, West +80.

**Step 2 — Divide by the starting value.** North: 60/200 = 30%. South: 30/50 = **60%**. West: 80/400 = 20%.

**Answer: South** — even though West had the biggest actual increase. Test writers love this contrast,
so always check which kind of change the question asks for.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Parts of a whole — pie charts and shares

A **pie chart** shows each category as a **percent of a total**. To turn a slice into a real amount,
you need that total (it's usually in the title or a note):

$$
\text{amount} = \text{percent} \times \text{total}
$$

**Comparing two slices?** Subtract the percents first, then multiply once. If Bus is 40% and Car is
30% of 400 students, the difference is 10% of 400 = 40 — one multiplication instead of two.

**Share of a total from a table:** add up the total, then divide: share = part ÷ total.
</Concept>

<Example title="From percents to people">
<DataChart c={{
  type: 'pie',
  title: 'How 400 Students Get to School',
  categories: ['Walk', 'Bus', 'Car', 'Bike'],
  series: [{ name: 'Share', values: [15, 40, 30, 15] }],
  unit: '%',
}} />

How many more students take the bus than walk?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Difference in percents.** Bus 40% − Walk 15% = 25%.

**Step 2 — Turn it into students.** 25% of 400 = 0.25 × 400 = **100** students.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. A percent of a percent, and averages

**Percent of a percent — multiply.** If 30% of a company's revenue comes from Europe, and 40% of
the *European* revenue comes from France, then France provides 0.30 × 0.40 = 0.12 = **12%** of the
total — not 40%. The 40% is a percent of Europe, not of everything.

Always ask: **"percent of what?"** Then chain the percents together.

**Averages from a display.** Add the values you need and divide by how many there are. To average
one row of a table over 3 years, add its 3 values and divide by 3.
</Concept>

<Example title="Two layers of percent">
A company has 2,000 employees. 45% of them work in sales, and 30% of the sales employees work remotely.
How many sales employees work remotely?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Sales employees.** 45% of 2,000 = 900.

**Step 2 — Remote sales employees.** 30% of 900 = **270**.

**Or in one step:** 0.45 × 0.30 × 2,000 = 0.135 × 2,000 = 270. So they're 13.5% of all employees.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — percent vs. percentage points">
If a rate goes from 10% to 15%, it rose by **5 percentage points** — but that's a **50% increase**
(5 ÷ 10). Read carefully: "increased by how many percentage points" and "increased by what percent" are
different questions.
</Gotcha>

## 5. Estimate and compare

Most data questions don't need exact arithmetic. **Look at the answer choices first.** If they're far
apart (like 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%), round the numbers and pick the closest.

**Comparing fractions quickly:**
- **Compare to a benchmark.** Is 198/612 more or less than ⅓? A third of 612 is 204, and 198 is less, so
  198/612 is a bit under ⅓.
- **Round both parts.** 289/1,012 ≈ 300/1,000 = 30%.
- **When the tops are close, the smaller bottom wins.** 60/150 is bigger than 60/240.
</Concept>

<Example title="Which share is bigger?">
Is 47/160 or 61/190 the larger fraction?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Estimate each as a percent.** 47/160: 10% of 160 is 16, so 30% is 48 — 47 is just under
30%. 61/190: 10% of 190 is 19, so 30% is 57 — 61 is a bit over 30% (about 32%).

**Answer: 61/190 is larger.** No long division needed.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Recap

| Question type | What to do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| any display | title, units, scale, footnotes first | "(thousands)": 18.0 → 18,000 |
| "by how much" | new − old | 50 → 80: +30 |
| "by what percent" | (new − old) ÷ old | 50 → 80: +60% |
| pie chart | percent × total; subtract percents first | 25% of 400 = 100 |
| percent of a percent | multiply them | 30% of 40% = 12% |
| "percentage points" | just subtract the percents | 10% → 15%: +5 points (a 50% increase) |
| far-apart choices | round and estimate | 289/1,012 ≈ 30% |
</Concept>
