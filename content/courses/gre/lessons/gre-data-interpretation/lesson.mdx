<Intuition>
Data interpretation questions come in small sets that share one table or graph. The math
is rarely beyond percents and averages; the difficulty is **reading the display correctly**
and doing that math quickly. Most errors come from misreading a label — thousands versus
millions, a percent of the total versus an actual count, the wrong year. Spend the first
fifteen seconds on the display itself, and the questions get much easier.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## Read the display first

Before any question, check:

1. **Title and units** — “sales in thousands of dollars,” “percent of total.”
2. **What each row and column means**, and whether rows sum to a total.
3. **Scales on graphs** — does the axis start at zero? Is it a percent or a count?
4. **Footnotes** — they often hold the one fact a question depends on.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## The three computations

- **Percent change** = (later − earlier) ÷ earlier.
- **Share of total** = part ÷ total. If the table gives percents of a total, multiply by
  the total to get counts.
- **Average across rows** = sum ÷ number of rows.

For “which is greatest” questions, compare ratios roughly before computing any exactly.
</Concept>

<Example title="Percent increase vs. absolute increase">
| Region | 2020 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| North | 200 | 260 |
| South | 50 | 80 |
| West | 400 | 480 |

Which region had the greatest **percent** increase from 2020 to 2022?

<Solution>
North: 60/200 = 30%. South: 30/50 = **60%**. West: 80/400 = 20%.

South wins even though West had the largest *absolute* increase (80). Test-writers love
that contrast — read which one the question asks for.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — percent of a percent">
If a graph shows that 30% of a company’s revenue comes from Europe and 40% of European
revenue comes from France, France provides 0.3 × 0.4 = 12% of total revenue — not 40%.
</Gotcha>
