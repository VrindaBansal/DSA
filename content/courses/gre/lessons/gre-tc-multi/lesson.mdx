<Intuition>
Multi-blank completions look intimidating, but they give you an advantage: each blank you
solve becomes evidence for the others. The skill is choosing where to start. Blank (i) is
often the hardest because it comes before the clues. Find the blank whose meaning the
sentence pins down most tightly, solve it, and let it unlock the rest. Then — because there
is **no partial credit** — read the whole completed sentence once to confirm it hangs
together.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## The format

Two-blank and three-blank questions give **three choices per blank**, in separate columns.
You pick one word per column. The question counts as right only if **every** blank is right.

The method:

1. Read the whole sentence.
2. **Start with the easiest blank** — the one with the most direct clue.
3. Predict, match, and treat the answer as a fixed part of the sentence.
4. Move to the next blank using everything you now know.
5. **Reread the complete sentence.** If it doesn’t make sense as a whole, one blank is wrong.
</Concept>

<Example title="Start where the clue is">
The committee’s chair, usually (i)___ in debate, astonished her colleagues by losing her
temper; her outburst, however, did not (ii)___ them, since they agreed her objections were
entirely (iii)___.

| Blank (i) | Blank (ii) | Blank (iii) |
|---|---|---|
| imperturbable | provoke | irrelevant |
| volatile | placate | pertinent |
| taciturn | confirm | frivolous |

<Solution>
**Start with (i).** “Astonished … by losing her temper” means losing her temper is unusual
for her, so she is usually calm: **imperturbable**. (*Volatile* is the trap — it describes
the outburst, not her usual self. *Taciturn* is about talking, not temper.)

**Then (iii).** Her colleagues “agreed” with her objections — so the objections were
relevant: **pertinent**.

**Then (ii).** Because they agreed with her, the outburst did not anger them: **provoke**.
(*Placate* reverses the logic — the outburst wasn’t trying to calm anyone.)

**Reread:** “usually imperturbable … did not provoke them, since … entirely pertinent.” ✓
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Structural patterns to recognize

- **Parallel pairs:** “once X, now Y” / “not X but Y” → the blanks are opposites.
- **Chains of cause:** “because X, Y” → the blanks agree in direction.
- **A blank that describes another blank:** “an act of (ii)___ that shocked auditors” after
  “(i) publicly generous but privately stealing” → (ii) names what (i) revealed.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — solving blank (i) in isolation">
Blank (i)’s choices often include both a word and its opposite, and both can look fine
until you read the rest of the sentence. If blank (i) feels like a coin flip, skip it,
solve a later blank, and come back.
</Gotcha>
