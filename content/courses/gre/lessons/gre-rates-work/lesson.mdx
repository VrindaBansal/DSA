<Intuition>
Speed, work, and mixture problems all run on the same idea: **a rate is an amount per unit of
time** (miles per hour, pages per minute, tanks per hour). Once you write down each rate, the
problems turn into simple multiplication and addition.

The two mistakes to avoid are also the same across all three: **averaging things that shouldn't
be averaged** (like two speeds, or two job times) and **losing track of units** (minutes vs.
hours). This lesson shows the safe way around both.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Distance = rate × time

$$
\text{distance} = \text{rate} \times \text{time}
$$

Cover the one you want and the other two tell you how to get it:
- distance = rate × time
- rate = distance ÷ time
- time = distance ÷ rate

**Units have to match.** If the rate is in miles per **hour**, the time must be in **hours**.
Convert minutes to hours by dividing by 60: 45 minutes = 45/60 = 0.75 hour; 20 minutes = ⅓ hour.
</Concept>

<Example title="Mixed units">
A bus travels 18 miles in 24 minutes. What is its speed in miles per hour?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Convert the time to hours.** 24 minutes = 24/60 = 0.4 hour.

**Step 2 — rate = distance ÷ time.** 18 ÷ 0.4 = **45 miles per hour**.

**Another way:** 24 minutes is 2/5 of an hour, so in a full hour the bus goes 18 × 5/2 = 45 miles. ✓

**The trap:** 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 — that's miles per *minute*.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 2. Average speed: total distance ÷ total time

If you drive to a city at 60 mph and back at 40 mph, your average speed is **not** 50 mph.
Why? You spend **more time** at the slower speed, so it counts for more.

**The only safe rule:**

$$
\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}
$$

Find each leg's time, add them, and divide the total distance by the total time. If the problem
doesn't give a distance, **pick a convenient one** — a number both speeds divide into.
</Concept>

<Example title="The round trip">
A driver goes from A to B at 60 mph and returns along the same road at 40 mph. What is the
average speed for the round trip?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Pick a distance.** No distance is given, so choose one that 60 and 40 both divide:
say A to B is **120 miles**.

**Step 2 — Time for each leg.** There: 120 ÷ 60 = 2 hours. Back: 120 ÷ 40 = 3 hours.

**Step 3 — Totals.** Distance: 240 miles. Time: 5 hours.

**Step 4 — Average speed.** 240 ÷ 5 = **48 mph** — not 50.

(Any distance gives 48. There's also a shortcut for equal distances:
2 × 60 × 40 ÷ (60 + 40) = 48.)
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — averaging averages">
Averaging two speeds only works if you spend **equal time** at each. When the **distances** are
equal (as in a round trip), the slower leg takes longer, so the average is pulled below the
midpoint. Always go back to total distance ÷ total time.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## 3. Two things moving at once

When two people or vehicles move at the same time, combine their speeds:

- **Toward each other** (or away from each other): the gap closes (or opens) at the **sum** of their
  speeds. Cars 300 miles apart driving toward each other at 70 and 50 mph close the gap at 120 mph:
  300 ÷ 120 = 2.5 hours.
- **Same direction, one chasing the other:** the gap closes at the **difference** of their speeds.
  A car at 70 mph gains on a car at 50 mph at 20 mph.

Then use **time = gap ÷ combined speed.**

If one starts later, first figure out **how big the gap is** when the second one starts.
</Concept>

<Example title="Catching up">
Train P leaves a station at noon traveling 60 mph. At 1:00 p.m., train Q leaves the same station
on a parallel track in the same direction at 80 mph. At what time does Q catch up?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — The gap when Q starts.** By 1:00, P has traveled 60 miles. So Q starts 60 miles behind.

**Step 2 — How fast Q closes the gap.** Same direction → subtract: 80 − 60 = 20 miles per hour.

**Step 3 — Time to close it.** 60 ÷ 20 = 3 hours after 1:00 → **4:00 p.m.**

**Check:** at 4:00, P has gone 4 × 60 = 240 miles and Q has gone 3 × 80 = 240 miles ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Work problems: add the rates

"Alex can paint a room in 6 hours. Bea can paint it in 3 hours. How long if they work together?"
The tempting answer is to average or add the times — both wrong. Instead, turn each time into a
**rate**: how much of the job gets done **per hour**.

- Alex: 1 room in 6 hours → **1/6 of a room per hour**.
- Bea: 1 room in 3 hours → **1/3 of a room per hour**.

**Working together, rates add:** 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = **1/2 room per hour**. So the room
takes **2 hours**.

$$
\text{time together} = \frac{1}{\text{rate}_1 + \text{rate}_2}
$$

A quick sanity check: working together must be **faster than the faster person alone**. (2 hours
beats Bea's 3.) For exactly two workers, there's a shortcut: time = (a × b) ÷ (a + b) = 18 ÷ 9 = 2.

**Working backward:** if you know the time together and one person's time, subtract rates to get
the other person's rate.
</Concept>

<Example title="Find one worker's time">
Working together, Kim and Lee can mow a lawn in 4 hours. Kim alone takes 6 hours. How long
would Lee take alone?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Rates you know.** Together: 1/4 of the lawn per hour. Kim: 1/6 per hour.

**Step 2 — Lee's rate is the difference.** 1/4 − 1/6 = 3/12 − 2/12 = 1/12 of the lawn per hour.

**Step 3 — Flip the rate into a time.** 1/12 per hour → **12 hours**.

Sanity check: Lee is slower than Kim, and together they're faster than either alone ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 5. Mixtures: track the pure ingredient

In a mixture problem, focus on **how much of the pure substance** there is — the salt, the acid,
the juice — not the percents.

$$
\text{amount of pure stuff} = \text{percent} \times \text{total amount}
$$

- 12 liters of a 30% acid solution contain 0.30 × 12 = **3.6 liters of acid**.
- **Adding water** changes the total but **not the amount of acid.**
- **Mixing two solutions:** the acid amounts add, and the totals add.

Set up one equation: (acid in the final mix) = (final percent) × (final total), then solve.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — adding pure water">
Diluting a solution doesn't lower its percent by the amount of water you add. Adding 12 liters of
water to 12 liters of 30% acid gives 3.6 liters of acid in 24 liters — **15%**, not "30% − something."
Always recompute from the amount of the pure ingredient.
</Gotcha>

<Example title="Diluting a solution">
How many liters of pure water must be added to 12 liters of a 30% acid solution to make a 20% acid
solution?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Acid stays fixed.** 0.30 × 12 = 3.6 liters of acid, before and after.

**Step 2 — Write the new mixture.** Adding w liters of water makes (12 + w) liters, and 20% of it is acid:
0.20 × (12 + w) = 3.6.

**Step 3 — Solve.** 12 + w = 3.6 ÷ 0.20 = 18, so w = **6 liters**.

**Check:** 3.6 liters of acid in 18 liters is 3.6/18 = 0.2 = 20% ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Example title="Mixing two solutions">
How many liters of a 10% salt solution must be mixed with 10 liters of a 40% salt solution to make a
20% salt solution?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Salt from each part.** From the 40% solution: 0.40 × 10 = 4 liters. From x liters of the
10% solution: 0.10x liters.

**Step 2 — The final mix.** It has 10 + x liters, and 20% of it is salt: 4 + 0.10x = 0.20(10 + x).

**Step 3 — Solve.** 4 + 0.1x = 2 + 0.2x → 2 = 0.1x → x = **20 liters**.

**Check:** salt = 4 + 2 = 6 liters in 30 liters = 20% ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>
