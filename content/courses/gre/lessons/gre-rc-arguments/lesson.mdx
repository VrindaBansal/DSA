<Intuition>
A few Verbal questions give you a short **argument** — a paragraph where someone draws a conclusion from
some evidence — and ask you to find its assumption, weaken it, or strengthen it.

These are among the most predictable questions on the test, because GRE arguments fail in a small
number of recurring ways. The key idea is the **gap**: the space between what the evidence actually
shows and what the conclusion claims. Almost every argument question is really asking one of three
things about that gap:

- **Assumption:** what fills the gap?
- **Weaken:** what makes the gap wider?
- **Strengthen:** what closes the gap?
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Evidence, conclusion, and the gap

Every argument has two parts:
- **Evidence** (premises) — the facts given.
- **Conclusion** — the claim the author wants you to accept.

**Find the conclusion first.** Words like *therefore, so, thus, concludes, should* usually flag it.
Then ask: **"Does the evidence really prove that?"** Whatever's missing is the **gap**.
</Concept>

<Example title="Find the gap">
*"Students who join the after-school reading club score higher on reading tests than students who
don't. Therefore, requiring every student to join the club would raise the school's reading scores."*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Conclusion.** "Requiring every student to join … would raise … reading scores." (Flagged by
*therefore*.)

**Step 2 — Evidence.** Club members score higher than non-members.

**Step 3 — The gap.** The evidence shows the club and high scores **go together** (a correlation). The
conclusion says the club **causes** high scores. Maybe strong readers are simply the ones who choose to
join a reading club!

**Step 4 — How questions would use it.**
- **Weaken:** "Students who join the club are mostly those who already read a lot at home."
- **Strengthen:** "In a study that randomly assigned students to the club, members improved more."
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Assumptions and the negation test

An **assumption** is something the argument needs to be true but never says. It's the bridge across the
gap.

**The negation test** tells you whether a choice is a required assumption:
1. Turn the choice into its **opposite**.
2. Ask: does the argument **fall apart**?
3. If yes, it was a required assumption. If the argument survives, it wasn't.
</Concept>

<Example title="The negation test">
*"Downloads of our new app doubled this quarter, so twice as many people are now using it."*

Is this an assumption: "The share of people who download the app and then actually use it didn't drop"?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Negate it.** "The share of downloaders who actually use the app dropped sharply."

**Step 2 — Does the argument survive?** No. If many new downloaders never open the app, doubling
downloads doesn't mean doubling users. The conclusion collapses.

**Step 3 — Verdict.** It **is** a required assumption.

**Compare a wrong choice:** "The app is free." Negate it: "The app costs money." The argument about
downloads and users is unaffected — so price wasn't an assumption.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Weaken and strengthen

Once you've found the gap:

- **To weaken,** find a choice that **widens the gap** — most often by offering **another explanation**
  for the evidence (a different cause, or a third factor behind both).
- **To strengthen,** find a choice that **closes the gap** — most often by **ruling out** another
  explanation.

For each choice ask: **"Does this make the conclusion more or less likely, given the evidence?"** A
choice that's merely on the same topic does neither.
</Concept>

<TradeoffTable id="gre-cr-types" />

<Example title="One argument, both directions">
*"After a company began offering free healthy snacks in its office, employee sick days fell by
10 percent that year. The manager concludes that the snacks improved employees' health."*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — The gap.** Sick days fell **after** the snacks arrived. The conclusion says the snacks
**caused** it. Something else might explain the drop.

**Step 2 — A weakener offers another explanation:** "That year's flu season was unusually mild across
the whole region." Now the drop might have happened anyway.

**Step 3 — A strengthener rules that out:** "At the company's other offices, which didn't offer
snacks, sick days did not fall that year." The mild-season explanation is weaker now.

**Step 4 — A distractor:** "Employees said they enjoyed the snacks." Enjoyment doesn't tell us whether
the snacks caused the drop in sick days.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. The recurring flaws

GRE arguments break in the same few ways again and again. Learn to recognize them:

| Flaw | What it looks like | How to weaken it |
|---|---|---|
| correlation → causation | A goes with B, so A causes B | a third factor, or B causes A |
| unrepresentative sample | a poll of cycling-blog readers → "the city thinks…" | show the sample differs from the whole group |
| bad comparison (analogy) | it worked in town X, so it'll work in Y | X and Y differ in a way that matters |
| ignoring the base rate | more vaccinated people got sick | far more people were vaccinated overall |
| mixing up measures | revenue rose, so profit rose | costs rose too |
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — the relevant-sounding wrong answer">
Wrong choices usually talk about the right **topic** without touching the **gap**: "the survey was
long," "the bike lanes will be painted green." Ask of every choice: does this change how well the
evidence supports the conclusion?
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## 5. Paradoxes — explain how both facts are true

Some questions give two facts that **seem** to clash and ask what would **explain** them. The right
answer doesn't deny either fact; it shows how **both can be true at once**, usually by adding a missing
piece of information.
</Concept>

<Example title="Explain the surprise">
*"The town's population grew by 15 percent over the past decade, yet enrollment in its public schools
fell."*

Which would best explain this?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — State the clash.** More people, but fewer students. Normally a growing town has more
children.

**Step 2 — What would make both true?** The newcomers must not be adding school-age children — or
something else is removing students.

**Step 3 — A good explanation:** "Most of the town's new residents are retirees." More people, fewer
kids — both facts hold.

**Step 4 — Weak answers:** "The schools hired more teachers" (doesn't explain fewer students);
"The town built new roads" (unrelated).
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Recap

| Question | What the right answer does |
|---|---|
| find the conclusion | the claim the evidence is meant to support (often after *so, therefore*) |
| assumption | fills the gap — negate it and the argument collapses |
| weaken | widens the gap — usually another explanation |
| strengthen | closes the gap — usually rules out another explanation |
| flaw | names the error: correlation ≠ cause, bad sample, bad comparison |
| paradox | shows how both facts can be true |
</Concept>
