<Intuition>
A few Verbal questions give you a short argument — a paragraph where someone draws a
conclusion from evidence — and ask you to find its assumption, weaken it, or strengthen it.
These are the most mechanical questions on the test, because arguments on the GRE fail in a
small number of recurring ways. Learn to see the **gap** between the evidence and the
conclusion, and nearly every argument question becomes: “which choice fills the gap, widens
it, or names it?”
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## Find the gap

Every argument has **premises** (the evidence) and a **conclusion** (the claim). Read the
conclusion first — words like *therefore, so, thus, concludes* flag it. Then ask: what
would have to be true for this evidence to prove that conclusion? That missing link is the
**assumption**, and the argument’s weakness lives there.
</Concept>

<Example title="Spot the gap">
*Students who join the after-school reading club score higher on reading tests than
students who don’t. Therefore, requiring every student to join the club would raise the
school’s reading scores.*

What is the gap?

<Solution>
The evidence is a **correlation** (club members score higher). The conclusion is
**causal** (the club raises scores). The gap: it assumes the club causes the higher scores,
rather than, say, strong readers choosing to join the club.

- A **weakener** attacks the gap: “Students who join are those who already read the most
  at home.”
- A **strengthener** fills it: “In a study that randomly assigned students to the club,
  members improved more.”
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## The negation test for assumptions

To check a possible assumption, **negate it**. If the negated version destroys the
argument, it was a required assumption.

Argument: “Sales of our new app doubled, so twice as many people are using it.”
Candidate assumption: “The share of downloaders who actually use the app didn’t fall.”
Negate it: “The share of downloaders who use the app fell a lot.” Now the conclusion
collapses — so it was a required assumption.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## The recurring flaws

| Flaw | Looks like | Weaken with |
|---|---|---|
| Correlation → causation | A goes with B, so A causes B | a third factor, or reverse causation |
| Unrepresentative sample | poll of blog readers → “the city thinks…” | the sample differs from the population |
| Bad analogy | it worked in town X, so it will work in Y | X and Y differ in a way that matters |
| Ignoring the base rate | more vaccinated patients got sick | far more people were vaccinated overall |
| Equivocation of measures | revenue up, so profit up | costs rose too |
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — the “relevant-sounding” wrong answer">
Wrong choices in weaken/strengthen questions usually discuss the right topic without
touching the gap: “bike lanes are painted green,” “the survey was long.” Ask of each
choice: does this make the *conclusion* more or less likely, given the *evidence*?
</Gotcha>
