<Intuition>
The Issue essay isn't judged on whether your opinion is "right" — there's no right answer. It's judged
on **how well you argue** for your position:

- Is your position **clear**?
- Do you support it with **reasons and specific examples**?
- Do you deal fairly with the **other side**?
- Is your writing **clear and organized**?

That makes it one of the most coachable parts of the test. A simple, reliable structure and a few
well-chosen examples will carry you most of the way.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The task — and why the instructions matter

You get one **prompt** (a claim about a broad topic like education, technology, government, science, or
the arts) followed by **specific instructions**. You have **30 minutes** and type in a basic editor with
no spellcheck.

**The instructions change from prompt to prompt,** and you must follow them. The main versions:

| The instructions say… | So your essay must… |
|---|---|
| consider ways the statement might or might not hold true | discuss when it's true **and** when it isn't |
| describe circumstances in which the recommendation would or would not be advantageous | give specific situations on both sides |
| address the most compelling reasons that could challenge your position | take on the strongest objections |
| discuss which view more closely aligns with your own, addressing both views | discuss **both** views, then pick one |
| discuss the claim **and the reason** on which it's based | evaluate the reason, not just the claim |
| consider the possible consequences of implementing the policy | discuss what would happen if the policy were adopted |

Essays that ignore the instructions lose points even when they're well written.

**Scoring:** each essay gets 0–6 (in half points) from a trained reader and a scoring program.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## 2. Take a position — usually a qualified one

Extreme positions ("always," "never," "completely") are hard to defend in 30 minutes. A **qualified**
position — agree, **but** with a limit or condition — is easier to support and shows the careful
thinking top scores reward.

**A formula that works:** "I largely agree/disagree **because** [main reason]. **However,** [the
condition or exception]."
</Concept>

<Example title="From a weak thesis to a strong one">
Prompt: *"Governments should place few, if any, restrictions on scientific research and development."*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Decide your basic side.** Mostly agree: open research drives progress.

**Step 2 — Find the exception.** Research that puts people at risk without their consent.

**Step 3 — Combine them into one thesis.**

- **Weak:** "I agree completely." (No reason, no nuance.)
- **Strong:** "I largely agree: open research drives progress, and restrictions usually do more harm
  than good. But when research poses direct risks to people who haven't consented to them, limited,
  specific restrictions are justified."

The strong version gives a **reason**, a **qualification**, and a preview of what the essay will argue.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Body paragraphs: reason + example + explanation

A reliable essay structure:

1. **Introduction (3–4 sentences):** put the issue in your own words, state your qualified position,
   preview your reasons.
2. **Body paragraph 1:** your strongest reason + a specific example, **explained**.
3. **Body paragraph 2:** a second reason + a different kind of example (history, science, business,
   your own observation).
4. **Counterargument paragraph:** the strongest opposing view, and your response (section 4).
5. **Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** restate your position and why it matters. No new ideas.

**The most important part of a body paragraph is the explanation.** An example doesn't argue for itself.
The sentences **after** the example must spell out how it supports your position.
</Concept>

<Example title="Building a body paragraph">
Using the research prompt above, write the core of one body paragraph.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Reason.** "Many of the most valuable discoveries came from research whose uses no one could
predict."

**Step 2 — Specific example.** "The mathematicians who developed the number theory behind modern
encryption weren't thinking about online banking."

**Step 3 — Explanation (the link back to the position).** "Had their work needed approval based on its
expected usefulness, it might never have been funded. Restrictions built on predictions of which
research will matter would cut off exactly the kind of work that later proves most important."

**Why it works:** one clear claim, a specific example, and an explanation that ties the example back to
the thesis.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Deal with the other side

Strong essays don't pretend the other side doesn't exist. They:

1. **State the strongest objection fairly** — the one a smart opponent would actually make.
2. **Respond to it directly.** Either show why it doesn't defeat your position, or show how your
   qualification already accounts for it.

Handling a counterargument well is one of the clearest differences between a 4 and a 6.
</Concept>

<Example title="A counterargument paragraph">
You argue that governments should place few restrictions on research. Write the counterargument and
your response.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — State the strongest objection.** "Critics argue that some research, such as work on
dangerous pathogens, is so risky that it must be tightly controlled."

**Step 2 — Concede what's true in it.** "That concern is serious, and it's exactly why my position
allows limited restrictions when research endangers people who haven't consented."

**Step 3 — Explain why your position still holds.** "But these cases are narrow. Using them to justify
broad restrictions would slow the vast majority of research, which carries no such danger."
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 5. The 30-minute plan

| Minutes | Do |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Read the prompt and instructions twice. Choose a qualified position. Jot down 2 reasons, 2 examples, and the strongest counterargument. |
| 5–27 | Write, following the structure. Don't stop to perfect every sentence. |
| 27–30 | Proofread for typos, missing words, and any sentence a reader might misread. |

**Plain, precise language wins.** Readers reward clear writing, not rare vocabulary.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — impressive words, empty argument">
"The multifaceted ramifications of this paradigm are indubitably consequential" says nothing.
"Restricting research would slow medical progress" says something. Always choose the second kind of
sentence.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## Recap

| Part | What it needs |
|---|---|
| instructions | read them twice; your essay must do what they ask |
| thesis | a clear side + a reason + a qualification |
| body paragraph | reason → specific example → explanation linking it to the thesis |
| counterargument | the strongest objection, stated fairly, then answered |
| conclusion | restate the position; no new ideas |
| timing | 5 plan · 22 write · 3 proofread |
</Concept>

<Cheatsheet>
Practice: write one essay a week from week 5, timed at 30 minutes. Ask the tutor to critique it against the rubric table above.
