<Intuition>
The Issue essay isn’t judged on whether your opinion is correct — there is no correct
answer. It is judged on **how well you argue** for a position: how clear it is, how well
you support it with reasons and examples, how you handle the other side, and how clearly
you write. That makes it one of the most coachable parts of the test. A simple, reliable
structure and a handful of well-chosen examples will carry you most of the way.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## The task

You get one prompt — a claim about a broad topic (education, technology, government, the
arts, science) — and specific instructions, such as “discuss the extent to which you agree
or disagree” or “address the most compelling reasons someone might disagree.” You have
**30 minutes** and type in a basic editor with no spellcheck.

Read the instructions carefully: they change from prompt to prompt, and essays that ignore
them lose points even when they are well written.

Essays are scored 0–6 (in half points) by a trained reader and a scoring program; the
section score combines them.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## Take a position — usually a qualified one

Extreme positions (“always,” “never”) are hard to defend. A **qualified** position — agree,
but with a condition — shows the nuance the top scores reward.

Prompt (example): *“Governments should place few, if any, restrictions on scientific
research and development.”*

- Weak: “I agree completely.”
- Strong: “I largely agree: open research drives progress, and restrictions usually do more
  harm than good. But when research poses direct risks to people who have not consented to
  them, limited, specific restrictions are justified.”
</Concept>

<Concept>
## The structure

1. **Introduction (3–4 sentences):** restate the issue in your own words, state your
   qualified position, preview your reasons.
2. **Body paragraph 1:** your strongest reason + one specific example, explained.
3. **Body paragraph 2:** a second reason + a different kind of example (history, science,
   business, a personal observation).
4. **Counterargument paragraph:** state the strongest opposing view fairly, then explain
   why your position still holds — or how your qualification accommodates it.
5. **Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** restate the position and why it matters. No new ideas.
</Concept>

<Example title="A body paragraph, built well">
Using the research prompt above, write the core of one body paragraph (reason + example +
explanation).

<Solution>
*“Many of the most valuable discoveries came from research whose applications no one could
foresee. The researchers who developed the mathematics behind modern encryption, for
instance, were pursuing abstract number theory, not online banking. Had their work required
approval based on its expected usefulness, it might never have been funded. Restrictions
built on predictions of which research will matter would, in effect, cut off exactly the
kind of work that later proves most important.”*

Why it works: **one** clear claim, a **specific** example, and — most important — an
**explanation** that ties the example back to the position. Examples don’t argue for
themselves; the sentences after them do.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## The 30-minute plan

| Minutes | Do |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Read the prompt and instructions twice. Choose a qualified position. List 2 reasons, 2 examples, and the strongest counterargument. |
| 5–27 | Write, following the structure. Don’t stop to perfect sentences. |
| 27–30 | Proofread: typos, missing words, and any sentence a reader might misread. |
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — impressive words, empty argument">
Readers reward clear, precise language, not rare vocabulary. A sentence like “The
multifaceted ramifications of this paradigm are indubitably consequential” says nothing.
“Restricting research would slow medical progress” says something. Choose the second kind.
</Gotcha>
