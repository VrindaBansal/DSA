<Intuition>
This is your word list: every word the practice bank's vocabulary, text completion, and sentence
equivalence questions draw on. It's organized the way the test thinks — words that mean the same thing
sit together, and each family points to its **opposite** family.

Don't try to learn it in one sitting. Come back daily, one or two families at a time, and let the vocab
drills and the review queue handle the repetition.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. How to study a family

Each card below is one family: a shared meaning, the words in it with short definitions, and (after
"≠") the name of its opposite family. For each family:

1. **Read the shared meaning first.** That's the one idea every word in the card hangs on.
2. **Read each word and its definition**, then **say a sentence** using it.
3. **Look up the opposite family** and skim it too. You'll learn two families for the price of one, and
   the opposite family is where the test's trap answers come from.
4. **Drill.** In the practice bank, open *Topic drills → Verbal* and use the four vocab drills: *what
   does it mean?*, *which word fits the definition?*, *synonyms*, and *antonyms*.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## 2. Families on a real question

Here's why families matter so much. In sentence equivalence, the right answer is **two words from the
same family**. And the test usually plants a **second pair** — from the **opposite** family — as a trap.
Once you think in families, you can see both pairs at a glance.
</Concept>

<Example title="Family thinking">
Select the **two** words that best complete the sentence and give it the same meaning:

"Far from being ___, the new mayor answered every question at length and seemed to enjoy the reporters'
company."

*taciturn · garrulous · reticent · hostile · loquacious · affable*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Find the clue.** The mayor "answered every question at length" and enjoyed the reporters —
talkative and sociable.

**Step 2 — Check the signal.** "Far from being ___" means the blank is the **opposite** of the clue. So
the blank means *saying little*.

**Step 3 — Sort the choices into families.**
- Saying little: **taciturn, reticent**
- Talkative: garrulous, loquacious
- No partner: hostile, affable

**Step 4 — Pick the family that matches.** **Taciturn** and **reticent** ✓.

**The trap:** *garrulous* and *loquacious* are a perfect pair — but they describe what the mayor
actually was, the opposite of what "far from" requires.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Opposite families

Antonym questions in the vocab drills — and many wrong answers on the real test — come straight from
opposite families. When you learn *ephemeral* (short-lived), learn its opposite family too: *enduring,
abiding, perennial, durable*.

**A useful habit:** for every word you study, ask "What's the opposite?" and name one word from the
opposite family. If you can, you know the word well.
</Concept>
