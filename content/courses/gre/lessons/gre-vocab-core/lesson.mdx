<Intuition>
This is your word list — every word the practice bank’s vocabulary, text completion, and
sentence equivalence questions draw on. It is organized the way the test thinks: words
that mean the same thing sit together, and each family points to its opposite. Don’t try
to learn it in one sitting. Come back to it daily, one or two families at a time, and let
the vocab drills and the review queue do the repetition.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## How to use this list

- **Work through one or two families a day.** Read each word and its definition, then say
  a sentence using it.
- **Learn the opposite family together with its partner** (it is named at the top right of
  each card after “≠”). When a test sentence turns on “although” or “far from,” the opposite
  family is where the trap answer comes from.
- **Then drill.** In the practice bank, open *Topic drills → Verbal* and use the four vocab
  drills: *what does it mean?*, *which word fits the definition?*, *synonyms*, and
  *antonyms*. Every explanation shows the word in a sentence, plus its family and opposites.
</Concept>

<Example title="Family thinking on a real question">
Select the **two** words that best complete the sentence and give it the same meaning:

“Far from being ___, the new mayor answered every question at length and seemed to enjoy
the reporters’ company.”

*taciturn · garrulous · reticent · hostile · loquacious · affable*

<Solution>
“Far from” means the blank is the **opposite** of what follows. What follows is talkative
and sociable, so the blank means *saying little*. Two words from that family:
**taciturn** and **reticent**.

Now notice the traps. *Garrulous* and *loquacious* are also a matching pair — from the
**opposite** family (talkative). They describe what the mayor actually did, which is
exactly why they are tempting. *Affable* (friendly) also describes the mayor, and
*hostile* has no partner.
</Solution>
</Example>
