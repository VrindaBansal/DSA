<Intuition>
Vocabulary is the biggest single lever on the Verbal section. About half of every Verbal section —
text completion and sentence equivalence — is really a vocabulary test, and reading questions depend on
it too.

The good news: the GRE doesn't test every word in the dictionary. The same few hundred words keep
coming back, and they fall into a few dozen **families** of words that mean nearly the same thing.
Learn the families — not an alphabetical list — and every word you learn helps you remember the others.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Learn words in families

An alphabetical list (*abate, aberrant, abscond…*) is the hardest way to learn: the words have nothing
to do with each other, so nothing reinforces anything. Instead, learn words that **mean the same
thing** together:

| Family | Words |
|---|---|
| stubborn | obstinate, intransigent, recalcitrant, obdurate |
| to praise | laud, extol, commend, acclaim |
| short-lived | ephemeral, transient, fleeting, evanescent |

**Why this works on the test:**
- Sentence equivalence asks you to find **two words that give the same meaning** — that's literally
  "find two members of the same family."
- If you only half-remember a word, knowing its **family** is usually enough to answer.

**Learn each family with its opposite.** *Stubborn* goes with *flexible* (pliant, malleable,
amenable). Wrong answers are very often built from the opposite family, so knowing both sides protects
you from the most common trap.
</Concept>

<Example title="Sort words into families">
Sort these eight words into two families, and name each family: *laud, disparage, extol, belittle,
commend, deride, acclaim, denigrate.*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Start with a word you know.** *Commend* means to praise. Which others sound like praise?
*Laud, extol, acclaim.*

**Step 2 — The rest should form the opposite family.** *Disparage, belittle, deride, denigrate* — all
mean to criticize or put down.

**Step 3 — Name them.**
- **Praise:** laud, extol, commend, acclaim.
- **Put down:** disparage, belittle, deride, denigrate.

Now each word is attached to a simple idea (praise / put down) and to its opposite family.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 2. Work out new words from their parts

You'll meet words you've never studied. Many GRE words are built from Latin and Greek pieces, and
knowing a dozen of the common ones often gets you close enough:

| Part | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| bene- | good | benevolent, beneficial, benefactor |
| mal- | bad | malevolent, malign, malicious |
| loqu-, locu- | speak | loquacious, eloquent, circumlocution |
| ver- | true | veracity, verify, verisimilitude |
| cred- | believe | credulous, incredulous, credible |
| a-, an- | not, without | amoral, anomalous, apathetic |
| im-, in- | not | impartial, intransigent, inscrutable |
| -phil- | love | philanthropic, bibliophile |
| pac-, plac- | peace, calm | pacify, placate, implacable |

**Always check your guess against the sentence.** Roots give you a starting point, not a guarantee.
</Concept>

<Example title="Decode a word">
You've never seen **implacable**. What does it probably mean? Check your guess in this sentence: "The
*implacable* critic dismissed every revision the author made."

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Split it into parts.** *im-* + *plac* + *-able*.

**Step 2 — Translate each part.** *im-* = not. *plac* = calm, peace (as in *placate*, to calm someone
down). *-able* = able to be.

**Step 3 — Put it together.** "Not able to be calmed" — someone you can't satisfy.

**Step 4 — Check the sentence.** A critic who dismisses **every** revision can't be satisfied ✓. So
*implacable* means impossible to calm or please.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Use a word's charge

Even when you can't define a word, you can often tell whether it's **positive** or **negative**.
*Pernicious*, *deleterious*, and *noxious* all *sound* bad — and they are.

This is powerful for elimination:

1. Decide whether the blank needs a **positive** or **negative** word.
2. Cross out every choice with the wrong charge.
3. Choose among what's left.

Often only one or two choices survive step 2.
</Concept>

<Example title="Eliminate by charge">
"The inspector's report was ___: it listed dozens of safety violations and recommended closing the
factory."

*glowing · damning · laudatory · favorable · effusive*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — What charge does the blank need?** Dozens of violations and a recommendation to close —
clearly **negative**.

**Step 2 — Cross out the positive words.** *Glowing, laudatory, favorable, effusive* are all praise.

**Step 3 — One word is left: damning** (strongly critical, showing someone is at fault). ✓

You didn't even need to be sure what *laudatory* or *effusive* meant — only that they're positive.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Easy words, unusual meanings

The GRE loves ordinary words used in a less common sense. If you know only the everyday meaning, you
fall right into the trap.

| Word | Everyday sense | GRE sense |
|---|---|---|
| qualify | be eligible | limit or soften a claim ("she qualified her praise") |
| check | inspect | hold back, restrain ("kept in check") |
| temper | anger | moderate, tone down ("tempered her criticism") |
| sanction | a penalty | *also* official approval — context decides |
| discriminating | biased | having good judgment, discerning |
| arrest | detain | stop or slow a process ("arrest the decline") |
| wanting | wishing for | lacking ("found wanting") |
| appreciate | be grateful | increase in value |
| economy | the economy | thrift, efficiency ("economy of language") |
| intimate | close | to hint ("she intimated that…") |

**The habit:** when a simple word appears in a sentence that seems to call for something harder, ask
whether the simple word has a second meaning that fits.
</Concept>

<Example title="A second meaning">
"The editor ___ her enthusiasm, warning that the book still needed a great deal of work."

*tempered · displayed · voiced · shared · exaggerated*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Find the clue.** "Warning that the book still needed a great deal of work" — she held her
enthusiasm back.

**Step 2 — Predict.** Toned down, limited.

**Step 3 — Match.** **Tempered** — in its GRE sense, "moderated" ✓. (If you only know *temper* as
"anger," you'd skip the right answer.)
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 5. A daily routine that works

1. **Learn 15–20 new words a day** from the word families in the next lesson — one or two whole
   families at a time.
2. **Use each word in a sentence of your own**, out loud or on paper. Producing a word fixes it in
   memory far better than just reading it.
3. **Drill in the practice bank.** Every vocab drill shows the word in a real sentence, with its family
   and its opposites, whether you get it right or wrong.
4. **Let the review queue handle repetition.** Words you miss come back after a day, then at growing
   intervals.

Fifteen to twenty minutes a day for eight weeks covers the whole core list several times.
</Concept>

<Check id="gre-vm-routine" />

<Gotcha title="Trap — recognizing isn't knowing">
"I've seen that word before" is not the same as knowing it. Test yourself: cover the definition and
say what the word means, then use it in a sentence. If you can't, it goes back in the review pile.
</Gotcha>
