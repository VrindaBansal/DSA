<Intuition>
Vocabulary is the single biggest lever on Verbal. Text completion and sentence
equivalence — roughly half of every Verbal section — are mostly vocabulary questions in
disguise, and reading comprehension leans on it too. The good news: the GRE’s vocabulary
is not infinite. The same few hundred words, grouped into a few dozen **meaning families**,
do most of the work. Learn the families, not a dictionary.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## Learn words in families

Alphabetical lists are the worst way to learn words: *abate, aberrant, abscond* have
nothing to do with each other, so nothing reinforces anything. Instead, learn words that
**mean the same thing together**:

- *obstinate, intransigent, recalcitrant, obdurate, intractable* — all “stubborn.”
- *laud, extol, acclaim, commend* — all “to praise.”
- *ephemeral, evanescent, transient, fleeting* — all “short-lived.”

This matches how the test works. Sentence equivalence asks you to find **two** words that
produce the same meaning — that is literally “find two members of the same family.” And
when you only half-remember a word, knowing its family is usually enough to answer.

Pair each family with its **opposite family** — *stubborn* vs. *flexible* (*pliant,
malleable, amenable*). Test-writers build wrong answers from opposites, so knowing both
sides of the pair protects you from the most common trap.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## Decode unfamiliar words: roots and charge

You will meet words you have never studied. Two tools usually get you close enough.

**Roots and prefixes.** Many GRE words are built from Latin and Greek parts:

| Part | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| bene- | good | benevolent, beneficial, benefactor |
| mal- | bad | malevolent, malign, malicious |
| loqu-, locu- | speak | loquacious, eloquent, circumlocution |
| ver- | true | veracity, verify, verisimilitude |
| cred- | believe | credulous, incredulous, credible |
| a-, an- | not, without | amoral, anomalous, apathetic |
| im-, in- | not | impartial, intransigent, inscrutable |
| -phil- | love | philanthropic, bibliophile |
| pac-, plac- | peace, calm | pacify, placate, implacable |

**Charge.** Even when you can’t define a word, you can often tell whether it is positive
or negative. *Pernicious, deleterious, noxious* all *sound* bad — and are. If a sentence
needs a negative word, eliminate every positive choice first.
</Concept>

<Example title="Decode it">
You have never seen the word **implacable**. Using parts, what does it probably mean? Then
check it in the sentence: “The *implacable* critic dismissed every revision the author
made.”

<Solution>
*im-* = not; *plac-* = calm, peace (as in *placate*); *-able* = able to be. So
**implacable** ≈ “not able to be calmed or appeased.”

The sentence confirms it: a critic who dismisses *every* revision cannot be satisfied.
That is exactly right — *implacable* means unable to be placated. Roots got you there
without a dictionary.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Secondary meanings: easy words, hard meanings

The GRE loves ordinary words used in uncommon senses. Readers who know only the everyday
meaning fall straight into the trap.

| Word | Everyday sense | GRE sense |
|---|---|---|
| qualify | be eligible | limit or modify a claim (“she qualified her praise”) |
| check | inspect | restrain, stop (“kept in check”) |
| temper | anger | moderate, soften (“tempered her criticism”) |
| sanction | penalty | *also* official approval — context decides |
| discriminating | biased | discerning, having good judgment |
| arrest | detain | stop or slow a process (“arrest the decline”) |
| wanting | desiring | lacking (“found wanting”) |
| appreciate | be grateful | increase in value |
| economy | the economy | thrift, efficiency (“economy of language”) |
| intimate | close | to hint or suggest (“she intimated that…”) |
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — the everyday meaning">
When a simple word appears as an answer choice in a sentence that seems to need a harder
word, stop and ask whether the simple word has a second meaning that fits. “The editor
___ her enthusiasm, warning that the book still needed work” → **tempered** (moderated),
not a word about anger.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## A daily routine that works

1. **20 new words a day** from the word families in the next lesson — one or two whole
   families at a time.
2. **Say each word in a sentence of your own** — out loud or written. Producing a word
   fixes it far better than recognizing it.
3. **Do vocab drills in the practice bank.** Each drill question shows the word in a real
   sentence, its family, and its opposite family, whether you get it right or wrong.
4. **Let the review queue handle repetition.** Words you miss come back after a day, then
   at growing intervals.

Fifteen to twenty minutes a day for eight weeks covers the whole core list several times.
</Concept>
