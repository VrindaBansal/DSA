<Intuition>
GRE geometry is less about memorizing dozens of theorems and more about using a **small toolkit**
over and over. For lines and triangles, the toolkit is:

- a straight line is **180°**;
- a triangle's angles add to **180°**;
- right triangles follow **a² + b² = c²**, plus two special shapes;
- similar triangles are **scaled copies** of each other.

Most questions combine two of these. One warning overrides everything: **figures are not
necessarily drawn to scale.** Trust what's stated, not what looks true.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Angles on lines and parallel lines

**Three basic facts:**
- Angles that make a **straight line** add to **180°**.
- Angles all the way **around a point** add to **360°**.
- When two lines cross, the **opposite** angles (called vertical angles) are **equal**.

**Parallel lines.** When a line (a *transversal*) crosses two parallel lines, it makes eight
angles — but they come in only **two sizes**: a small one (call it a) and a big one (b). Every small
angle equals every other small angle, and **a + b = 180°**.

So if you know **one** angle in a parallel-lines figure, you know **all eight**: the ones that look
the same size are equal, and a small one plus a big one make 180°.
</Concept>

<Example title="Parallel lines with algebra">
Lines ℓ and m are parallel and are crossed by a transversal. One of the acute angles measures
(2x + 10)° and one of the obtuse angles measures (4x + 50)°. What is x?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Pick the right fact.** An acute (small) angle and an obtuse (big) angle in a
parallel-lines figure add to 180°.

**Step 2 — Write the equation.** (2x + 10) + (4x + 50) = 180, so 6x + 60 = 180.

**Step 3 — Solve.** 6x = 120, so **x = 20**.

**Check:** the angles are 50° and 130°, and 50 + 130 = 180 ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Angles in a triangle

- The three angles of any triangle add to **180°**.
- **Exterior angle:** if you extend one side, the outside angle equals the **sum of the two
  far-away inside angles**. (Because both it and those two angles "complete" the same third angle
  to 180°.)
- **Isosceles** triangle (two equal sides): the angles **opposite** the equal sides are equal.
  **Equilateral** (three equal sides): every angle is 60°.
- The **biggest angle is across from the longest side**, and the smallest angle is across from the
  shortest side.

**Isosceles questions often have two cases.** If an isosceles triangle has a 40° angle, that 40° could
be one of the two equal angles (making the angles 40°, 40°, 100°) **or** the odd one out (making
40°, 70°, 70°). Unless the question says which, consider both.
</Concept>

<Example title="Angles in a ratio">
The angles of a triangle are in the ratio 2 : 3 : 7. What is the largest angle?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Count the parts.** 2 + 3 + 7 = 12 parts.

**Step 2 — Find one part.** The angles total 180°, so one part = 180° ÷ 12 = 15°.

**Step 3 — The largest has 7 parts.** 7 × 15° = **105°**.

**Check:** 30° + 45° + 105° = 180° ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 3. The Pythagorean theorem

In a **right triangle**, the two sides that form the right angle are the **legs** (a and b), and the
longest side, across from the right angle, is the **hypotenuse** (c):

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

Legs 6 and 8: 36 + 64 = 100, so the hypotenuse is √100 = 10.

**Save time with triples** — right triangles with whole-number sides. The GRE uses them constantly,
often scaled up (6-8-10 is 3-4-5 doubled). If you spot one, you can skip the arithmetic:
</Concept>

<Example title="Find the hypotenuse two ways">
A right triangle has legs of length 15 and 20. How long is the hypotenuse?

<Solution>
**The long way.** 15² + 20² = 225 + 400 = 625, and √625 = **25**.

**The fast way.** 15 : 20 is the same as 3 : 4 (both divided by 5). So this is a 3-4-5 triangle times 5,
and the hypotenuse is 5 × 5 = **25**.
</Solution>
</Example>

**45-45-90** — sides **x : x : x√2**. This is half of a square, cut along its diagonal. So a square
with side s has diagonal **s√2**. Going the other way, divide the diagonal by √2 to get the side.

**30-60-90** — sides **x : x√3 : 2x**. The shortest side (x) is across from 30°, and the
hypotenuse is **twice** the shortest side. This triangle is half of an equilateral triangle, which is
why it shows up whenever an equilateral triangle does.

**How to use them:** find the shortest side x first, then build the other sides from it.
</Concept>

<Example title="Area of an equilateral triangle">
An equilateral triangle has sides of length 8. What is its area?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Draw the height.** A line from the top straight down to the base cuts the triangle into
two 30-60-90 triangles. Each has hypotenuse 8 and short leg 4 (half the base).

**Step 2 — Find the height.** The height is the long leg: x√3 with x = 4, so height = **4√3**.

**Step 3 — Area.** ½ × base × height = ½ × 8 × 4√3 = **16√3** (about 27.7).

In general, an equilateral triangle with side s has area s²√3 / 4. Here 64√3 / 4 = 16√3 ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 5. Which lengths can make a triangle?

Imagine trying to build a triangle from sticks. If two sticks are 4 and 9, the third can't be tiny
(the 4 and it couldn't reach across the 9) and can't be huge (the 4 and 9 couldn't reach its ends).
The rule:

**The third side is between the difference and the sum of the other two** — strictly between.

With sides 4 and 9: 9 − 4 = 5 and 9 + 4 = 13, so **5 < third side < 13**. A side of exactly 5 or 13
would make a flat line, not a triangle.
</Concept>

<Example title="Which could be the third side?">
Two sides of a triangle are 3 and 8. Which of 4, 5, 6, or 11 could be the third side?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Difference and sum.** 8 − 3 = 5 and 8 + 3 = 11.

**Step 2 — Write the range.** 5 < third side < 11.

**Step 3 — Test each.** 4 is too small. 5 and 11 are the endpoints — not allowed. Only **6** works.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 6. Area, and similar triangles

**Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height.** The height must be **perpendicular** to the base. Any side
can be the base, as long as you use the height that goes with it. In a right triangle, the two legs
are a base and height already: legs 6 and 8 give area ½ × 6 × 8 = 24.

**Similar triangles** have the same angles, so one is a scaled-up copy of the other. You'll usually
spot them when two triangles share an angle and have another pair of equal angles — for example, when
a line inside a triangle is **parallel** to one side, it cuts off a smaller similar triangle.

If the sides of the bigger triangle are **k times** the sides of the smaller:
- every **length** (sides, heights, perimeter) is **k** times as big;
- the **area** is **k²** times as big (area uses two lengths multiplied together).

Sides twice as long → area 4 times as big. Sides 3 times as long → area 9 times as big.
</Concept>

<Example title="A parallel line makes similar triangles">
In triangle ABC, point D is on AB and point E is on AC, with DE parallel to BC. AD = 4, DB = 6, and
DE = 5. What is BC?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Spot the similar triangles.** DE is parallel to BC, so triangle ADE is a smaller copy of
triangle ABC (they share angle A, and the parallel lines make the other angles equal).

**Step 2 — Find the scale.** Match up a pair of sides: AD = 4 in the small triangle and AB = 4 + 6 = 10
in the big one. So k = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5.

**Step 3 — Scale DE.** BC = 2.5 × 5 = **12.5**.

**Bonus:** the big triangle's area is 2.5² = 6.25 times the small one's.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — trusting the figure">
A triangle that *looks* like a right triangle, or lines that *look* parallel, are not known to be
unless the question says so (or marks the right angle with a little square). On quantitative
comparison, that uncertainty often makes the answer "cannot be determined."
</Gotcha>

## Recap

| Fact | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| straight line | angles add to 180° | x + 3x = 180 → x = 45 |
| parallel lines | only two angle sizes; small + big = 180° | 70° and 110° |
| triangle | angles add to 180°; exterior angle = the two far angles added | far angles 40° and 60° → exterior 100° |
| isosceles | equal sides ↔ equal angles; check both cases | 40° → 40-40-100 or 40-70-70 |
| right triangle | a² + b² = c² | 15-20-25 is 3-4-5 × 5 |
| 45-45-90 | x : x : x√2 | square with side 5 has diagonal 5√2 |
| 30-60-90 | x : x√3 : 2x | equilateral side 8 → height 4√3 |
| third side | difference < side < sum | 4 and 9 → between 5 and 13 |
| similar, ratio k | lengths × k, area × k² | sides × 3 → area × 9 |
</Concept>
