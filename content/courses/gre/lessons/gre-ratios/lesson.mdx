<Intuition>
A ratio compares amounts. "The ratio of red to blue marbles is 5 : 3" doesn't tell you how
many marbles there are — it tells you that for every 5 red there are 3 blue. There might be
5 and 3, or 10 and 6, or 50 and 30.

The single most useful way to think about a ratio is as **parts**: the jar is made of
**equal-sized parts**, 5 of them red and 3 of them blue. Almost every ratio question on the GRE
becomes easy once you ask **"how big is one part?"**
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. What a ratio tells you

A ratio can be written three ways: **5 : 3**, **5 to 3**, or **5/3**. Order matters: "red to blue"
is 5 : 3; "blue to red" is 3 : 5.

**Part-to-part vs. part-to-whole.** A ratio of red to blue of 5 : 3 compares **parts**. The
**whole** has 5 + 3 = **8 parts**. So:
- red is **5/8** of the marbles, and blue is **3/8** of them;
- red to total is 5 : 8.

This is the step people skip: a ratio of 5 : 3 does **not** mean red is 5/3 of the jar. To get a
fraction of the whole, first add the parts.

**Simplifying a ratio** works like simplifying a fraction: divide both numbers by the same thing.
12 : 18 = 2 : 3. With decimals or fractions, multiply to clear them: 0.5 : 2 = 1 : 4, and
½ : ⅓ = 3 : 2 (multiply both by 6).
</Concept>

<Example title="Ratio to fraction">
In a club, the ratio of members who are students to members who are not is 2 : 7. What fraction
of the club's members are students?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Count all the parts.** 2 + 7 = 9 parts.

**Step 2 — Students are 2 of those parts.** Fraction = **2/9**.

**The trap:** answering 2/7. That compares students to non-students, not students to everyone.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 2. The parts method

When a problem gives a ratio plus **one real number** — a total, or a difference, or one of the
amounts — do this:

1. Write the ratio as parts, and figure out how many parts the real number stands for.
2. Divide to find **one part**.
3. Multiply to get whatever the question asks for.

$$
\text{one part} = \frac{\text{real amount}}{\text{number of parts it represents}}
$$

The real number can be the **total** (use the sum of the parts), a **difference** (use the
difference of the parts), or **one of the amounts** (use its own parts).
</Concept>

<Example title="Ratio and a total">
A school has 720 students, and the ratio of juniors to seniors is 4 : 5. How many seniors are there?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Total parts.** 4 + 5 = 9 parts make 720 students.

**Step 2 — One part.** 720 ÷ 9 = 80 students.

**Step 3 — Seniors are 5 parts.** 5 × 80 = **400** seniors (and 320 juniors — check: 400 + 320 = 720 ✓).
</Solution>
</Example>

<Example title="Ratio and a difference">
The ratio of red to blue marbles in a jar is 5 : 3, and there are 14 more red marbles than blue.
How many marbles are in the jar?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — What does 14 stand for?** The difference between red and blue: 5 − 3 = **2 parts**.

**Step 2 — One part.** 14 ÷ 2 = 7 marbles.

**Step 3 — The whole jar is 8 parts.** 8 × 7 = **56** marbles (35 red, 21 blue — and 35 − 21 = 14 ✓).
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Combining two ratios

"a : b = 2 : 3 and b : c = 4 : 5." You'd like one ratio a : b : c, but the two ratios use
**different-sized parts** for b (3 in one, 4 in the other). Fix that first:

1. Find the shared term (here, b).
2. Scale each ratio so the shared term is the **same number** — use the least common multiple
   of 3 and 4, which is 12.
3. Read off the combined ratio.

- a : b = 2 : 3 → multiply by 4 → **8 : 12**
- b : c = 4 : 5 → multiply by 3 → **12 : 15**

So **a : b : c = 8 : 12 : 15**, and a : c = 8 : 15.
</Concept>

<Example title="Chain two ratios">
If x : y = 3 : 4 and y : z = 6 : 5, what is x : z?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — The shared term is y.** It's 4 in the first ratio and 6 in the second; the LCM is 12.

**Step 2 — Scale.** x : y = 3 : 4 → × 3 → 9 : 12. And y : z = 6 : 5 → × 2 → 12 : 10.

**Step 3 — Combine.** x : y : z = 9 : 12 : 10, so **x : z = 9 : 10**.

**The trap:** reading 3 and 5 straight off the two ratios (3 : 5) without matching y first.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Ratios that change: find what stays the same

"The ratio of boys to girls is 3 : 5. Then 6 more boys join, and the ratio becomes 1 : 1." Two
things change (the boys and the ratio), but **one amount doesn't: the girls.** That's the key to
every changing-ratio problem.

**Method A — use a variable for one part.** Boys = 3k, girls = 5k. After 6 boys join:
3k + 6 = 5k, so k = 3. Girls = 15, boys = 9 (then 15).

**Method B — scale the ratios so the unchanged amount matches.** Girls are 5 parts before and 1
part after. Rewrite 1 : 1 as 5 : 5 so girls are 5 parts in both. Boys went from 3 parts to 5 parts
— up 2 parts — and that's the 6 new boys. So one part = 3, and girls = 5 × 3 = 15.

Both methods work; pick whichever feels more natural.
</Concept>

<Example title="A changing ratio">
A fruit bowl holds apples and oranges in the ratio 4 : 3. After 5 apples are eaten, the ratio is 3 : 3
(that is, 1 : 1). How many oranges are in the bowl?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — What stays the same?** The oranges.

**Step 2 — Use k for one part.** Apples = 4k, oranges = 3k.

**Step 3 — Write the new situation.** Apples after eating: 4k − 5. Now apples = oranges:
4k − 5 = 3k, so k = 5.

**Step 4 — Answer.** Oranges = 3k = **15** (apples went from 20 to 15 ✓).
</Solution>
</Example>

## 5. Proportions and scaling

A **proportion** says two ratios are equal. If 3 notebooks cost $4.50, then 7 notebooks cost x,
where

$$
\frac{3}{4.50} = \frac{7}{x}
$$

**Cross-multiply:** 3x = 7 × 4.50 = 31.50, so x = $10.50. Or use the **unit rate**: one notebook
costs $1.50, so 7 cost $10.50. The unit rate is usually faster.

**Direct vs. inverse.** Before scaling, ask: when one thing goes up, does the other go **up** or
**down**?
- **More means more (direct):** more notebooks → more cost. Multiply the same way.
- **More means less (inverse):** more workers → **fewer** days to finish a job. Here the
  **total work** stays the same: workers × days = constant.
</Concept>

<Example title="More workers, fewer days">
8 workers can paint a building in 15 days. Working at the same rate, how many days would 12
workers take?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Direct or inverse?** More workers finish **faster**, so it's inverse.

**Step 2 — Find the total work.** 8 workers × 15 days = **120 worker-days** of painting.

**Step 3 — Divide by the new crew.** 120 ÷ 12 = **10 days**.

**The trap:** setting up 8/15 = 12/x, which gives 22.5 days — more workers taking *longer*.
Always ask whether the answer should go up or down before you compute.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — scaling the wrong way">
Before cross-multiplying, predict the direction. If more machines should mean less time, an
answer bigger than the original time is wrong — no matter how neat the arithmetic looks.
</Gotcha>

## Recap

| Situation | Do this | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ratio → fraction | add the parts for the whole | 2 : 7 → students are 2/9 |
| ratio + total | total ÷ (sum of parts) = one part | 720, 4 : 5 → one part = 80 |
| ratio + difference | difference ÷ (difference of parts) | 14 more, 5 : 3 → one part = 7 |
| two ratios | match the shared term with its LCM | 3 : 4 and 6 : 5 → 9 : 12 : 10 |
| changing ratio | find the amount that doesn't change | 4k − 5 = 3k |
| proportion | unit rate or cross-multiply | 3 for $4.50 → 7 for $10.50 |
| more means less | keep the product constant | 8 × 15 = 12 × 10 |
</Concept>
