<Intuition>
"Percent" literally means **"per hundred."** 30% is 30 out of every 100 — the fraction 30/100,
or the decimal 0.30. That's the whole idea; everything else in this lesson is about using it
without tripping.

Percent questions trip people up in two predictable ways: dividing by the **wrong starting
number**, and assuming that percents **add** when they actually multiply. We'll build one
tool — the **multiplier** — that handles both.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Percents, decimals, and fractions

To switch forms:

- **Percent → decimal:** divide by 100 (move the decimal point two places left). 35% = 0.35; 4% = 0.04; 0.5% = 0.005.
- **Decimal → percent:** multiply by 100. 0.125 = 12.5%; 1.5 = 150%.
- **Percent → fraction:** put it over 100 and simplify. 35% = 35/100 = 7/20.
- **Fraction → percent:** divide, then × 100. 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%.

**Doing percents in your head.** Build them from easy pieces:
- **10%**: move the decimal one place left. 10% of 240 = 24.
- **1%**: move it two places. 1% of 240 = 2.4.
- **5%** is half of 10%; **20%** is double 10%; **25%** is a quarter; **50%** is half.
- So 15% of 240 = 10% + 5% = 24 + 12 = **36**.

A handy fact: **x% of y = y% of x.** 8% of 25 is hard; 25% of 8 is 2. Same answer.
</Concept>

<Example title="Convert and compute">
(a) Write 3/8 as a percent. (b) Write 12.5% as a fraction. (c) What is 35% of 60?

<Solution>
**(a)** 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375, and 0.375 × 100 = **37.5%**.

**(b)** 12.5% = 12.5/100 = 125/1,000 = **1/8** (divide top and bottom by 125).

**(c)** Build it: 10% of 60 = 6, so 30% = 18, and 5% = 3. 35% = 18 + 3 = **21**.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Translating percent sentences

Almost every percent sentence can be turned into an equation word by word:

| word | becomes |
|---|---|
| **percent** | ÷ 100 (or × 0.01) |
| **of** | × (multiply) |
| **is**, **was**, **equals** | = |
| **what**, **what number** | x (the unknown) |

There are only three kinds of basic percent questions, depending on which piece is missing:

- **Find the part:** "What is 30% of 70?" → x = 0.30 × 70 = 21.
- **Find the percent:** "12 is what percent of 48?" → 12 = (x/100) × 48 → x = 25.
- **Find the whole:** "15 is 20% of what number?" → 15 = 0.20 × x → x = 75.

A quick sanity check: the number after **"of"** is the whole — the base the percent is taken from.
</Concept>

<Example title="Find the whole">
27 is 45% of what number?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Translate.** "27 **is** 45% **of** what number" → 27 = 0.45 × x.

**Step 2 — Solve.** x = 27 ÷ 0.45 = 2,700 ÷ 45 = **60**.

**Check:** 45% of 60 = 0.45 × 60 = 27 ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 3. Percent change: divide by the starting value

When something goes from an old value to a new one:

$$
\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%
$$

The key is the bottom: always divide by the **original** (the "from" value). A price that goes
from $40 to $50 rose by $10, and $10 out of the original $40 is **25%**.

**"More than" and "less than" are not symmetric.** If A = 50 and B = 40:
- A is **25% more** than B (the difference, 10, divided by B = 40).
- B is **20% less** than A (the same 10, divided by A = 50).

Whatever comes after "than" is the number you divide by.
</Concept>

<Example title="Percent increase and decrease">
A laptop's price rose from $800 to $920. Later it fell from $920 back to $800. What were the
percent increase and the percent decrease?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Increase.** Change = $120. Divide by the starting value, $800: 120/800 = 0.15 = **15%**.

**Step 2 — Decrease.** Change = $120 again, but now the starting value is $920:
120/920 ≈ 0.130 = about **13%**.

Same dollar change, different percents — because the starting values differ. This is why a
15% rise followed by a 15% drop does **not** get you back to where you started.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 4. Multipliers: the one tool you need

Increasing something by 20% leaves you with the original 100% plus 20% more: 120% of it.
So **"increase by 20%" means "multiply by 1.20."** Likewise, **"decrease by 20%" means
"multiply by 0.80"** — you keep 80%.

| change | multiply by |
|---|---|
| increase by p% | 1 + p/100 (e.g., +8% → × 1.08) |
| decrease by p% | 1 − p/100 (e.g., −35% → × 0.65) |

**Changes in a row multiply — they don't add.** A 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease is
1.10 × 0.90 = **0.99** — a 1% loss overall, not zero. The second change is 10% of a *bigger*
number, so it takes away more than the first added.

To read the result: a total multiplier of 1.32 is a 32% increase; 0.91 is a 9% decrease.
</Concept>

<Example title="Successive changes">
A store raises a price by 25%, then puts the new price on sale for 20% off. How does the sale
price compare with the original?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Write the multipliers.** +25% → × 1.25. −20% → × 0.80.

**Step 2 — Multiply them.** 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00.

**Answer: the sale price equals the original price exactly.** Try it with $100: $100 → $125 → 20% off
$125 is $25 → $100 ✓. (Adding the percents, +25 − 20 = +5%, would give the wrong answer.)
</Solution>
</Example>

## 5. Working backward to the original

"After a 25% increase, the price is $60. What was the original price?" It's tempting to take
25% off $60 — but the 25% was of the **original**, not of $60.

Use the multiplier instead: original × 1.25 = 60, so **original = 60 ÷ 1.25 = 48**.

**Rule: to undo a percent change, divide by its multiplier.** (Never subtract the same percent.)
</Concept>

<Example title="Finding the original">
After a 25% increase, a bicycle costs $60. What did it cost before the increase?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Write the equation.** original × 1.25 = 60.

**Step 2 — Divide.** original = 60 ÷ 1.25 = **48**.

**Check:** 25% of 48 = 12, and 48 + 12 = 60 ✓.

**The trap:** 60 − 25% of 60 = 60 − 15 = 45. That undoes the change using the wrong base.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 6. Percent of a percent, and percentage points

**Percent of a percent — multiply.** "40% of the students play a sport, and 25% of those play
soccer." Soccer players are 25% **of 40%** of the students: 0.25 × 0.40 = 0.10 = **10%** of all
students.

**Percentage points vs. percent.** When a rate itself is a percent, there are two ways to
describe its change:
- A rate that goes from 5% to 8% rises by **3 percentage points** (just subtract: 8 − 5).
- The same change is a **60% increase** (the change, 3, divided by the original, 5).

GRE answer choices often include both, so read which one the question asks for.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — percent vs. percentage points">
A tax rate rising from 10% to 12% is a 2 **percentage point** increase but a **20%** increase.
If the question says "by what percent," divide the change by the original rate.
</Gotcha>

<Example title="Percent of a percent">
At a company, 60% of the employees are women, and 25% of the women are managers. What percent
of all employees are women managers?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Multiply the percents.** 25% of 60% = 0.25 × 0.60 = 0.15.

**Step 2 — Convert.** 0.15 = **15%** of all employees.

**Check with 100 employees:** 60 women; 25% of 60 = 15 women managers; 15 out of 100 = 15% ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 7. Simple and compound interest

**Simple interest** is the same every year: a fixed percent of the **original** amount.
$1,000 at 5% simple interest earns $50 per year, so $150 in 3 years.

$$
\text{simple interest} = \text{principal} \times \text{rate} \times \text{years}
$$

**Compound interest** earns interest on the interest, so each year's growth is a multiplier:
$1,000 at 5% compounded annually for 3 years grows to

$$
1{,}000 \times 1.05^3 \approx 1{,}157.63
$$

That's $157.63 of interest — a little more than simple interest's $150, because years 2 and 3
earn interest on earlier interest.

If interest compounds more than once a year, split the rate: 8% compounded quarterly means
2% per quarter, 4 times a year.
</Concept>

<Example title="Simple vs. compound">
$2,000 is invested for 2 years at 10% annual interest. How much more does it earn if the
interest is compounded annually than if it's simple interest?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Simple:** $2,000 × 0.10 × 2 = **$400**.

**Step 2 — Compound:** $2,000 × 1.10 × 1.10 = $2,000 × 1.21 = $2,420, so the interest is **$420**.

**Step 3 — Difference:** $420 − $400 = **$20**. (It's exactly 10% of the first year's $200 interest,
earned in year 2.)
</Solution>
</Example>

## Recap

| Situation | Do this | Example |
|---|---|---|
| percent ↔ decimal | move the point two places | 7.5% = 0.075 |
| mental percents | build from 10%, 5%, 1% | 15% of 80 = 8 + 4 = 12 |
| a sentence | "of" = ×, "is" = =, "what" = x | 12 = x% × 48 → 25% |
| percent change | (new − old) ÷ **old** | 40 → 50: +25% |
| more than / less than | divide by the number after "than" | 50 is 25% more than 40; 40 is 20% less than 50 |
| one change | multiply by 1 ± p/100 | +8% → × 1.08 |
| several changes | multiply the multipliers | +10% then −10% → × 0.99 |
| back to the original | divide by the multiplier | 60 ÷ 1.25 = 48 |
| percent of a percent | multiply | 25% of 40% = 10% |
| percentage points | subtract the rates | 5% → 8% is +3 points (+60%) |
| compound interest | × (1 + r) each period | 1,000 × 1.05³ |
</Concept>
