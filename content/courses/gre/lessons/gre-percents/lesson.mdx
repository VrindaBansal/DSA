<Intuition>
Almost every percent question becomes easy once you stop thinking of percents as
something to add and subtract and start thinking of them as **multipliers**. A 20%
increase multiplies by 1.2. A 15% decrease multiplies by 0.85. Two changes in a row?
Multiply the two multipliers. Going backward to the original? Divide by the multiplier.
That one idea removes the classic traps: adding percents that don’t add, and taking a
percent of the wrong base.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## Translation

“Percent” means “per hundred,” “of” means multiply, and “is” means equals.

- 30% of 80 = 0.30 × 80 = 24
- 12 is what percent of 48? → 12 = (p/100) × 48 → p = 25
- 15% of what number is 9? → 0.15 × N = 9 → N = 60
</Concept>

<Concept>
## Percent change: always divide by the original

**Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.**

From 40 to 50 is +25% (10 ÷ 40). From 50 to 40 is −20% (10 ÷ 50). Same 10-unit change,
different percents, because the base differs.
</Concept>

<Example title="Percent change">
A jacket’s price rose from $80 to $92. By what percent did it increase?

<Solution>
Change = $12. Divide by the ORIGINAL price: 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15 → **15%**.

The trap is dividing by the new price: 12 ÷ 92 ≈ 13%.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Multipliers do all the work
</Concept>

<Example title="Successive changes">
A price rises 25% and then falls 20%. What is the net change?

<Solution>
Multiply the factors: 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00 → **no change**.

Adding the percents (+25 − 20 = +5%) is the trap. The 20% decrease is taken of a larger
number (after the increase), so it removes more than the 20% of the original.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Check id="gre-pct-successive" />

<Example title="Finding the original">
After a 25% discount, a lamp costs $54. What was the original price?

<Solution>
A 25% discount leaves 75% of the original: 0.75 × P = 54 → P = 54 ÷ 0.75 = **$72**.

Check: 25% of $72 is $18, and $72 − $18 = $54 ✓. The trap — adding 25% of $54 — gives
$67.50, because the discount was 25% of the *original*, not of the sale price.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Interest

- **Simple interest:** I = P × r × t. The interest each year is the same.
- **Compound interest (annually):** A = P(1 + r)ᵗ. Each year’s interest is earned on the
  previous interest too, so compound always beats simple after the first year.

$1,000 at 10% for 2 years: simple → $1,200; compound → $1,000 × 1.1² = $1,210.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — percent vs. percentage points">
A rate rising from 20% to 25% has gone up **5 percentage points**, which is a
**25 percent** increase. When a question compares rates, check which one it means.
</Gotcha>
