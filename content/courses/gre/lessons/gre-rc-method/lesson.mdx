<Intuition>
Reading comprehension is about half of every Verbal section, and it's the most learnable part.

The test isn't asking what **you** think about the topic, or what you know about it from somewhere
else. It asks what **this passage** says and what it clearly implies. That means:

- every right answer can be **pointed to** in the text, and
- every wrong answer either says something the passage doesn't, says it **too strongly**, twists what
  it says, or answers a different question.

Train yourself to **prove** each answer with a sentence from the passage, and reading questions stop
being a matter of opinion.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Read for the map, not the details

On your first read, aim to answer three questions, in a few words each:

1. **What's the author's main point?** (Often near the start or the end.)
2. **What does each paragraph (or each part) do?** For example: "describes a problem," "gives the usual
   solution," "raises an objection," "draws a conclusion."
3. **What's the author's attitude?** Neutral? Critical? Supportive but with doubts?

**Don't try to memorize** numbers, names, or examples. You'll go back for those when a question asks.

**Watch for pivot words** — *however, yet, but, although, in fact*. They usually mark where the author's
real view begins.
</Concept>

<Example title="Map a passage">
*Cities are often several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside. Dark surfaces absorb sunlight
and release it slowly, and the scarcity of vegetation reduces cooling. Planting street trees is
frequently proposed as a remedy, and in many cases it helps. Yet trees are not a universal solution: in
dry cities, watering them may strain water supplies, and in narrow streets a dense canopy can trap
exhaust. Planners therefore increasingly combine trees with other measures suited to local conditions.*

Write the map: main point, what each part does, and the author's attitude toward trees.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — What does each part do?**
- Sentences 1–2: **the problem** (cities are hotter) and **why** (dark surfaces, few plants).
- Sentence 3: **the usual remedy** — trees — which "in many cases" helps.
- Sentence 4, starting with the pivot **"Yet"**: **the limits** of trees.
- Sentence 5: **the conclusion** — combine trees with other measures.

**Step 2 — Main point.** Trees help with city heat but aren't a fix everywhere, so planners combine them
with other measures.

**Step 3 — Attitude toward trees.** Supportive, but with reservations.

With that map you can answer "main point," "attitude," and "why does the author mention…" questions
without rereading — and you know exactly where to look for details.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Question types — and where their answers live

Figure out **what kind** of question you're facing before you look at the choices. Each type sends you
to a different place in the passage:
</Concept>

<TradeoffTable id="gre-rc-question-types" />

<Concept>
**Inference questions** deserve extra care. "It can be inferred…" means: what **must** be true, based on
the passage — just **one small step** beyond what it says. The right answer is usually worded
cautiously ("may," "some," "can"). If a choice needs an extra assumption to be true, it's too far.
</Concept>

<Example title="An inference question">
Using the city-heat passage above:

"It can be inferred from the passage that planting a dense row of trees along a narrow, busy street
could…"

- (A) lower the temperature of the whole city
- (B) worsen air quality at street level
- (C) strain the city's water supply in any climate
- (D) make dark surfaces absorb less sunlight
- (E) be the most effective remedy available

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Find the relevant sentence.** "In narrow streets a dense canopy can trap exhaust."

**Step 2 — Take one small step.** Trapped exhaust at street level means worse air there.

**Step 3 — Check each choice against the text.**
- (A) Too big a claim — the passage never says trees cool a whole city.
- **(B)** Follows directly from "trap exhaust" ✓.
- (C) The water problem is only mentioned for **dry** cities, not "any climate."
- (D) Nothing links trees to how surfaces absorb sunlight.
- (E) The passage says the opposite — trees aren't a universal solution.

**Answer: (B).**
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Prove it: the four kinds of wrong answer

For every choice you're considering, **point to the sentence that supports it.** If you can't, cross it
off. Wrong answers almost always fall into one of four types:

| Type | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Out of scope** | sounds reasonable, but the passage never says it |
| **Too extreme** | the passage says "some," "may," or "often"; the choice says "all," "always," or "never" |
| **Distortion** | uses the passage's own words but twists or reverses the claim |
| **True but doesn't answer** | an accurate detail that doesn't answer the question asked (common in main-idea questions: too narrow) |

Learning to name the type makes elimination fast and confident.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — outside knowledge">
If a passage about bees leaves out something you know to be true, the answer still comes from the
passage. A choice that's "true in real life" but not stated or implied in the text is **out of scope**,
and it's wrong.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## 4. The special formats

**Select one or more** (three choices, "consider each choice separately"):
- Judge each choice **on its own**, as a true/false question about the passage.
- One, two, or all three can be right, and you must pick **all** the right ones — no partial credit.

**Select a sentence** (on the real test, you click a sentence in the passage; here it appears as a
choice of sentences):
- Look for the sentence that **does the job** the question names ("presents evidence against…",
  "states the author's conclusion") — not a sentence that just mentions the same topic.
</Concept>

## Recap

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| first read | main point, each part's job, author's attitude; watch pivot words |
| question type | main idea, detail, inference, function, vocabulary, select-all |
| go back | find the sentence that answers it |
| prove it | every kept choice needs a supporting sentence |
| eliminate | out of scope, too extreme, distortion, true but doesn't answer |
| inference | one small, cautious step beyond the text |
</Concept>
