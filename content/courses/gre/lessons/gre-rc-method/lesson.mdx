<Intuition>
Reading comprehension is about half of every Verbal section, and it is the most learnable
part. The test isn’t asking what you think about the topic, or what you know about it from
elsewhere. It asks what **this passage** says and implies. Every right answer can be pointed
to in the text. Every wrong answer either says something the passage doesn’t, says it too
strongly, or answers a different question. Train yourself to **prove** answers, and RC stops
being a matter of opinion.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## Read for the map, not the details

On your first read, aim to answer three questions, in a few words each:

1. **What is the author’s main point?** (Often in the first or last sentences.)
2. **What does each paragraph do?** (“presents the old view,” “raises an objection,”
   “gives evidence for the new view.”)
3. **What is the author’s attitude?** (Neutral? Critical? Supportive but with reservations?)

Don’t memorize numbers, names, or examples. You’ll come back for them when a question asks.
Watch for **pivot words** — *however, yet, but, although, in fact* — which usually mark the
author’s real position.
</Concept>

<Example title="Map a passage">
*Cities are often several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside. Dark surfaces
absorb sunlight and release it slowly, and the scarcity of vegetation reduces cooling.
Planting street trees is frequently proposed as a remedy, and in many cases it helps. Yet
trees are not a universal solution: in arid cities, irrigation may strain water supplies,
and in narrow streets a dense canopy can trap exhaust. Planners therefore increasingly
combine trees with other measures suited to local conditions.*

Write the map: main point, what each part does, and the author’s attitude to trees.

<Solution>
- **Main point:** trees help with urban heat but aren’t a universal fix, so planners
  combine them with other measures.
- **Structure:** the problem (cities are hotter, and why) → the common remedy (trees, which
  help) → the pivot (“Yet”): limitations → the conclusion (combine measures).
- **Attitude:** supportive but with reservations.

With that map you can answer primary-purpose, attitude, and function questions without
rereading, and you know exactly where to look for details.
</Solution>
</Example>

## Prove it: the four kinds of wrong answer

1. **Out of scope** — true-sounding, but the passage never says it.
2. **Too extreme** — the passage says “some” or “may”; the choice says “all” or “always.”
3. **Distortion** — uses the passage’s words, but reverses or garbles the claim.
4. **True but irrelevant** — accurate detail, but it doesn’t answer the question asked
   (common in main-idea questions: too narrow).

For every choice you keep, point to the sentence that supports it. If you can’t, it’s gone.
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — outside knowledge">
If a passage about coral reefs says something you know is incomplete, the answer still
comes from the passage. Choices that are “true in real life” but not stated or implied in
the text are wrong.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## The special formats

- **Select one or more** (three choices, “consider each choice separately”): judge each
  choice on its own. One, two, or all three may be right, and you need all of them.
- **Select in passage:** you click the sentence that does something. Here it appears as a
  choice of sentences. Look for the sentence that does the job named — not one that merely
  mentions the same topic.
</Concept>
