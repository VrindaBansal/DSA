<Intuition>
Sentence equivalence gives you one blank and six choices, and you must pick **exactly
two** that each complete the sentence **and** produce sentences that mean the same thing.
The good news: that requirement is a massive hint. The right answers are almost always
two members of the same word family. The bad news: test-writers know you know that, so
they plant a *second* synonym pair that doesn’t fit the sentence. The method below handles
both at once.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## The method

1. **Cover the choices** and predict the blank from the clue, exactly as in text completion.
2. **Look for words that match your prediction.** You need two.
3. **Confirm they are a pair** — the two completed sentences must mean essentially the same
   thing.
4. **Check the other pairs.** If you spot another synonym pair, make sure it doesn’t fit
   the clue better.

Both choices must be right to earn credit; there is no partial credit.
</Concept>

<Example title="Pair, and the fake pair">
The playwright’s later work, with its sparse dialogue and bare stages, is strikingly ___
compared with the lavish productions of her early career.

*austere · ornate · spartan · florid · derivative · acclaimed*

<Solution>
**Clue:** “sparse dialogue and bare stages,” contrasted with “lavish.” **Prediction:** plain,
stripped down.

Matching words: **austere** and **spartan** — both mean severely plain. ✓

The trap: **ornate** and **florid** are also a matching pair — they mean elaborately
decorated, which describes the *early*, lavish work. A pair has to fit the clue, not just
match each other. *Derivative* (unoriginal) and *acclaimed* (praised) have no partners.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Check id="gre-se-pair" />

<Gotcha title="Trap — the lone word that fits perfectly">
Sometimes one choice fits the sentence beautifully but has no partner among the choices.
It can’t be right: you must pick two words that produce the same meaning. If your favorite
word has no synonym in the list, your prediction may be too narrow — look for the pair.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## When you don’t know some words

Group what you know into pairs first. The remaining unknown words are often the answer —
especially if a known word that matches your prediction has no partner among the known
words. Words that merely *look* alike are not
automatically a pair — each one must fit the sentence on its own.
</Concept>
