<Intuition>
Sentence equivalence gives you **one blank and six choices**. You pick **exactly two** — and both must
(1) fit the sentence and (2) make sentences that **mean the same thing**.

That second rule is a huge hint: the right answers are almost always **two words from the same
family** (two synonyms). But test-writers know that, so they usually plant a **second synonym pair
that doesn't fit**. The method below handles both at once.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The method

1. **Cover the choices.** Find the clue and signal, and **predict** the blank in your own words —
   exactly as in text completion.
2. **Find the words that match your prediction.** You need two.
3. **Check they're a pair.** Put each one in the sentence. Do the two sentences mean essentially the
   same thing?
4. **Check the other pairs.** If you spot another synonym pair among the choices, make sure it doesn't
   fit the clue better.

You need **both** words right to get credit — there's no partial credit.
</Concept>

<Example title="The real pair and the fake pair">
"The playwright's later work, with its sparse dialogue and bare stages, is strikingly ___ compared with
the lavish productions of her early career."

*austere · ornate · spartan · florid · derivative · acclaimed*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Predict.** Clue: "sparse dialogue and bare stages," contrasted with "lavish." Prediction:
**plain, stripped down**.

**Step 2 — Find the matches.** **Austere** and **spartan** both mean severely plain.

**Step 3 — Check they're a pair.** "Strikingly austere" and "strikingly spartan" mean the same thing ✓.

**Step 4 — Check the other pair.** *Ornate* and *florid* are also a pair — they mean elaborately
decorated. But that describes the **early**, lavish work, not the later work. A pair has to fit the
clue, not just match each other. *Derivative* (unoriginal) and *acclaimed* (praised) have no partners.

**Answer: austere, spartan.**
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 2. The two traps

**Trap 1 — the fake pair.** Two choices that mean the same thing as each other, but **don't fit the
sentence**. Usually they're the **opposite** of what the blank needs, or they describe the wrong side of
a contrast. Matching each other isn't enough; the pair must match your prediction.

**Trap 2 — the lone word.** One choice fits the sentence beautifully but has **no partner** among the
other five. It can't be right, because you must pick two words that give the same meaning. If your
favorite word has no synonym in the list, your prediction may be too narrow — look for the pair that
fits.
</Concept>

<Example title="Both traps at once">
"Critics found the memoir surprisingly ___: the famously boastful actor admits his failures and credits
others for his success."

*modest · candid · arrogant · humble · vain · tedious*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Predict.** Clue: a boastful actor who admits failures and credits others. "Surprisingly"
means the blank contrasts with "boastful." Prediction: **not boastful — modest**.

**Step 2 — Find the pair.** **Modest** and **humble** ✓.

**Step 3 — Spot the traps.**
- **Fake pair:** *arrogant* and *vain* mean the same thing — but they describe his **reputation**, the
  opposite of what "surprisingly" requires.
- **Lone word:** *candid* (honest) fits the sentence well — admitting failures is honest — but there's
  no other word here meaning "honest." Without a partner, it can't be one of the two answers.

**Answer: modest, humble.**
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. When you don't know some of the words

1. **Group the words you know into pairs** (and note which ones have no partner).
2. **Cross off** any known pair that doesn't fit your prediction, and any known word with no partner.
3. If a **known word matches your prediction** but has no partner among the known words, its partner
   is probably one of the **unknown** words.
4. Use roots and charge to check the unknown word, if you can.

**Careful:** words that merely *look* alike (*credible / credulous*) aren't automatically a pair — each
one must fit the sentence on its own.
</Concept>

<Example title="Pair a known word with an unknown one">
"The new manager's instructions were so ___ that the team spent the week arguing about what she
actually wanted."

*explicit · vague · unambiguous · nebulous · tedious · lengthy*

Suppose you don't know *nebulous*.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Predict.** The team argued about what she wanted — the instructions were **unclear**.

**Step 2 — Pair up the known words.** *Explicit* and *unambiguous* are a pair meaning **clear** — the
opposite of the prediction ✗. *Tedious* and *lengthy* are about length, not clarity ✗.

**Step 3 — A known word fits: vague.** But it has no known partner. So its partner is likely the one
unknown word: *nebulous*.

**Step 4 — Confirm.** *Nebulous* comes from *nebula*, a cloud — cloudy, hazy, unclear ✓.

**Answer: vague, nebulous.**
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Recap

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| predict | cover the choices; clue + signal → your own word |
| find two | both must match the prediction |
| pair check | both sentences must mean the same thing |
| fake pair | a synonym pair that doesn't fit the clue — reject it |
| lone word | fits well but has no partner — can't be right |
| unknown words | a known word that fits with no known partner → its partner is probably the unknown word |
</Concept>
