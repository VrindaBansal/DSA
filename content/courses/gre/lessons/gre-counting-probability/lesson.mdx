<Intuition>
Almost every counting problem comes down to one idea: **if you make one choice in m ways and then a
second choice in n ways, there are m × n ways to make both.** Three shirts and four pairs of pants give
3 × 4 = 12 outfits.

Everything else in counting is that idea plus one question: **does the order matter?** Picking a
president and a vice-president (order matters — they're different jobs) is different from picking a
two-person team (order doesn't matter — it's the same team either way).

Probability then builds on counting: it's **the number of outcomes you want divided by the number of
possible outcomes**.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The slot method

Draw one blank **slot** for each choice you have to make. Write how many options each slot has, then
**multiply**.

A code of 2 letters followed by 3 digits (repeats allowed):

| slot | letter | letter | digit | digit | digit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| options | 26 | 26 | 10 | 10 | 10 |

26 × 26 × 10 × 10 × 10 = **676,000** codes.

**Two rules that prevent most mistakes:**
- **No repeats allowed?** Each slot has one fewer option than the one before.
- **A slot has a restriction** (must be odd, can't be 0)? **Fill that slot first**, then the others.
</Concept>

<Example title="Three-digit numbers with no repeated digit">
How many three-digit numbers have three different digits?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Draw three slots:** hundreds, tens, units.

**Step 2 — Restricted slot first.** The hundreds digit can't be 0, so it has **9** options (1–9).

**Step 3 — Next slot.** The tens digit can be anything except the digit already used: 10 − 1 = **9**
options (0 is allowed now).

**Step 4 — Last slot.** The units digit can't match either one used: 10 − 2 = **8** options.

**Step 5 — Multiply.** 9 × 9 × 8 = **648**.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Arrangements — when order matters

**Arranging all n things in a row** uses the slot method: n choices for the first spot, n − 1 for the
next, and so on down to 1. That product is written **n!** ("n factorial"):

$$
n! = n \times (n - 1) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1
$$

5 books on a shelf: 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120 ways.

**Arranging only k of the n things** (like awarding 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place): use just k slots.
From 10 runners: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720.

**Letters that repeat.** To arrange the letters of a word with repeated letters, start with the
arrangements as if every letter were different, then **divide by the factorial of each letter's
count** (swapping identical letters doesn't make a new arrangement).
</Concept>

<TradeoffTable id="gre-counting" />

<Example title="Arranging the letters of LEVEL">
How many different arrangements are there of the letters in LEVEL?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Count the letters.** 5 letters: L appears 2 times, E appears 2 times, V once.

**Step 2 — Pretend they're all different.** 5! = 120.

**Step 3 — Divide out the repeats.** The two L's can swap (2! = 2 ways) and the two E's can swap
(2! = 2 ways) without changing anything: 120 ÷ (2 × 2) = **30**.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Groups — when order doesn't matter (combinations)

A **committee**, a **team**, a **hand of cards**: Ann, Ben, and Cal make the same group however
you list it. Count these with **combinations**, written C(n, k), "n choose k":

$$
C(n, k) = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!}
$$

**Where the formula comes from:** count as if order mattered, then divide by the number of ways to
order the k people you picked (k!), because each group got counted that many times.

**The quick way to compute it:** multiply k numbers counting down from n, then divide by k!.
C(10, 3) = (10 × 9 × 8) ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 720 ÷ 6 = 120.

**Choosing from two separate groups** (like 2 teachers **and** 3 students): count each choice
separately, then **multiply**.
</Concept>

<Example title="Officers vs. a committee">
A club has 8 members. In how many ways can it choose (a) a president, a secretary, and a treasurer;
(b) a 3-person committee?

<Solution>
**(a) Different jobs, so order matters.** Three slots: 8 × 7 × 6 = **336**.

**(b) A committee is just a group, so order doesn't matter.** In part (a), every group of 3 people
was counted once for each way to hand out the 3 jobs — 3! = 6 times. So divide: 336 ÷ 6 = **56**.
That's C(8, 3).
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Restrictions: together, apart, at least one

**"Must be together":** glue them into one block. Arrange the blocks, then multiply by the ways to
arrange the people inside the block.
5 people in a row with A and B together: the block AB plus 3 others is 4 things → 4! = 24, and A and
B can swap inside the block → × 2 = **48**.

**"Must not be together":** it's easier to count the opposite and subtract.
Not together = total − together = 5! − 48 = 120 − 48 = **72**.

**"At least one":** count everything, then subtract the groups that have **none**.

$$
\text{at least one} = \text{total} - \text{none}
$$
</Concept>

<Example title="At least one">
A committee of 4 is chosen from 6 men and 3 women. How many committees include at least one woman?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — All committees.** 9 people, choose 4: C(9, 4) = (9 × 8 × 7 × 6) ÷ 24 = 126.

**Step 2 — Committees with no women** (all 4 from the 6 men): C(6, 4) = (6 × 5 × 4 × 3) ÷ 24 = 15.

**Step 3 — Subtract.** 126 − 15 = **111**.

(Counting "exactly 1 woman + exactly 2 + exactly 3" separately also works, but takes three times as
long.)
</Solution>
</Example>

## 5. Probability basics

When all outcomes are equally likely:

$$
P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{outcomes you want}}{\text{all possible outcomes}}
$$

A probability is always between **0** (impossible) and **1** (certain).

**The complement:** the chance something does **not** happen is **1 − P(it happens)**.

**"At least one" → use 1 − P(none).** It's the probability version of the counting trick above.
The chance of at least one six in 2 rolls of a die is 1 − P(no sixes) = 1 − (5/6)(5/6) = 1 − 25/36 = 11/36.

**Two dice:** there are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely outcomes. List the ones you want.
</Concept>

<Example title="A sum with two dice">
Two fair six-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum is 7?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Total outcomes.** 6 × 6 = 36.

**Step 2 — List the ones you want.** (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1) — 6 outcomes.
(Order matters here: rolling 1 then 6 is a different outcome from 6 then 1.)

**Step 3 — Divide.** 6/36 = **1/6**.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 6. AND and OR

**AND — both happen.** Multiply. If the events are **independent** (one doesn't affect the other):
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Two coin flips both heads: ½ × ½ = ¼.

**Without replacement,** the first draw changes what's left for the second, so update the second
fraction before multiplying.

**OR — at least one happens.** Add, then subtract the overlap (it was counted twice):

$$
P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)
$$

If A and B can't both happen, the overlap is 0 and you just add.
</Concept>

<Example title="Drawing without replacement">
A bag has 4 red and 6 blue marbles. Two are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that
both are red?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — First draw.** 4 red out of 10: 4/10.

**Step 2 — Second draw.** One red is gone, so 3 red out of 9 left: 3/9.

**Step 3 — AND, so multiply.** (4/10) × (3/9) = 12/90 = **2/15**.

(With replacement, the second draw would still be 4/10: (4/10)² = 4/25.)
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — adding for “and”">
"Both" means **multiply**. Adding gives a number that's too big — and it can even go over 1. If a
probability comes out bigger than 1, something went wrong.
</Gotcha>

## 7. Overlapping groups

When some people belong to **both** groups, adding the groups counts those people twice. Subtract them
once:

$$
\text{Total} = A + B - \text{Both} + \text{Neither}
$$

**When there are two different yes/no categories** (like men/women and glasses/no glasses), make a
**2×2 table**. Rows and columns must add up, so you can fill in the blanks one at a time.
</Concept>

<Example title="A 2×2 table">
Of 60 employees, 25 are managers. 36 employees work remotely, and 10 of the managers do **not** work
remotely. How many non-managers do not work remotely?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Set up the table** with what you know.

| | remote | not remote | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| manager | ? | 10 | 25 |
| non-manager | ? | **?** | ? |
| total | 36 | ? | 60 |

**Step 2 — Fill in what the totals force.** Not-remote total: 60 − 36 = 24. Non-manager total:
60 − 25 = 35.

**Step 3 — Answer.** Non-managers not remote = not-remote total − managers not remote = 24 − 10 = **14**.

**Check:** remote managers = 25 − 10 = 15, remote non-managers = 35 − 14 = 21, and 15 + 21 = 36 ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

## Recap

| Situation | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a sequence of choices | slots; multiply; restricted slot first | 3-digit, no repeats: 9 × 9 × 8 = 648 |
| arrange all n | n! | 5 books: 120 |
| arrange k of n | k slots counting down | top 3 of 10: 720 |
| repeated letters | n! ÷ (repeat counts)! | LEVEL: 120 ÷ 4 = 30 |
| choose a group | C(n, k) = count down k numbers ÷ k! | C(8, 3) = 56 |
| together / apart | block it / total − together | 5 people: 48 / 72 |
| at least one | total − none (or 1 − P(none)) | 126 − 15 = 111 |
| probability | wanted ÷ possible | sum of 7: 6/36 |
| AND / OR | multiply / add − overlap | red, red without replacement: 4/10 × 3/9 |
| overlapping groups | total = A + B − both + neither | or a 2×2 table |
</Concept>
