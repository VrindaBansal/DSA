<Intuition>
The GRE isn't an intelligence test or a school exam. It's a **standardized reasoning test with a fixed
menu of question types** — and every one of them can be learned.

The people who improve most aren't the ones who study hardest in general. They're the ones who learn
exactly what each question type rewards, practice that move until it's automatic, and stop losing points
to the **format** itself: running out of time, leaving blanks, or getting stuck on one hard question.

This lesson is the map. Everything else in the course fills it in.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The test in one table

Since September 2023, the GRE General Test takes **about 1 hour 58 minutes**. You write one essay, then
answer two Verbal Reasoning sections and two Quantitative Reasoning sections.
</Concept>

<Concept>
Three facts from that table drive most of your strategy:

- **Verbal and Quant are each scored 130–170**, in 1-point steps. Programs usually look at these two
  numbers first. Analytical Writing is scored separately, **0–6 in half points**.
- **The pace is tight but fair.** Verbal gives you about **1.5 minutes** per question; Quant about
  **1.75**. That's enough for careful work — as long as you don't sink five minutes into one question.
- **There's no penalty for a wrong answer.** A blank and a wrong answer both score zero, so a guess can
  only help.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## 2. The test adapts by section

The GRE adapts **by section, not by question**. How you do on the **first** Verbal section decides
whether your second Verbal section is easier or harder. The same goes for Quant. The harder second
section is where high scores come from — its questions count for more.

What that means for you:

1. **The first section of each subject matters most.** Give it full focus. A sloppy first section limits
   what the second can do for you.
2. **A hard second section is good news.** If the second section suddenly feels harder, you probably did
   well on the first. Don't panic — the difficulty is a sign a high score is still in reach.

Inside a section you can **skip questions, mark them, and come back**, and you can change answers until
time runs out. Nothing forces you to go in order.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## 4. Three rules that follow from the format

1. **Never leave a blank.** Before time runs out, click an answer on every question — even a pure guess.
2. **Bank time on the easy ones.** Every question in a section counts the same, so a quick right answer
   is worth exactly as much as a slow one.
3. **Move on after about 2½ minutes with no plan.** Mark it, guess, and come back if time remains. One
   stubborn question can cost you three easy ones.

You'll practice these rules in the timed sections of the practice bank, which run at exactly this pace.
</Concept>

<Example title="Why the format matters">
Two students take the same 15-question Quant section. Each can solve 11 of the questions and has no idea
how to do the other 4 (all five-choice multiple choice).

- **Student A** works in order, gets stuck for five minutes on one of the hard four, runs out of time,
  and never reaches 2 of the questions she could have solved. She leaves everything she didn't solve
  blank.
- **Student B** skips each hard question when it comes up, solves all 11 she can, then goes back and
  clicks a guess on the 4 hard ones.

How many more questions should Student B expect to get right?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Student A.** She reaches only 9 of her 11 solvable questions, and her blanks score nothing.
Expected score: **9**.

**Step 2 — Student B.** All 11 solvable questions, plus 4 guesses with a 1-in-5 chance each:
4 × ⅕ = 0.8. Expected score: **11.8**.

**Step 3 — Compare.** About **3 more questions on one section** — with exactly the same knowledge.
Across two Quant and two Verbal sections, format habits alone can be worth several points.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — leaning on the calculator">
Quant has an on-screen calculator, and it's slow: you click every button with a mouse. Most GRE
arithmetic is designed to be easier by hand or by estimating. Use the calculator for ugly arithmetic
you've already set up — never as a way to avoid thinking.
</Gotcha>
