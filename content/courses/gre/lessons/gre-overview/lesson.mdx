<Intuition>
The GRE is not an intelligence test and not a school exam. It is a **standardized
reasoning test with a fixed menu of question types**, and every one of them can be
learned. People who improve the most are not the ones who study hardest in general —
they are the ones who learn exactly what each question type rewards, practice that
move until it is automatic, and stop losing points to the format itself: running out
of time, leaving blanks, or getting stuck on one hard question. This lesson is the map.
Everything else in the course fills it in.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## The test in one table

Since September 2023 the GRE General Test takes **about 1 hour 58 minutes**. You write
one essay, then answer two Verbal Reasoning sections and two Quantitative Reasoning
sections.
</Concept>

<TradeoffTable id="gre-sections" />

<Concept>
Three facts from that table drive most of your strategy:

- **Verbal and Quant are scored 130–170**, in 1-point steps. Programs usually look at
  these two numbers first. Analytical Writing is scored separately, **0–6 in half points**.
- **Pace is tight but not brutal.** Verbal gives you about 1.5 minutes per question;
  Quant about 1.75. That is enough for careful work — if you don’t sink five minutes
  into one question.
- **There is no penalty for a wrong answer.** A blank and a wrong answer both score
  zero, so a guess can only help you.
</Concept>

<Concept>
## Section-level adaptivity

The GRE adapts **by section, not by question**. Your performance on the *first* Verbal
section determines whether your second Verbal section is easier or harder; the same is
true for Quant. A harder second section is where the high scores live — questions in it
are worth more toward your final score.

Two consequences:

1. **The first section of each subject matters most.** Treat it with full focus. A sloppy
   first section caps what the second can do for you.
2. **A hard second section is good news.** If the second section suddenly feels harder, it
   usually means you did well on the first. Don’t panic — that difficulty is the sign that
   a high score is still available.

Inside a section you can **skip questions, mark them, and come back**, and you can change
answers until time runs out. Use that: nothing forces you to do the questions in order.
</Concept>

## The question types

<Example title="Why the format matters">
Two students take the same 15-question Quant section. Each can solve 11 of the questions
and has no idea how to do the other 4 (all five-choice multiple choice).

Student A works in order, gets stuck for five minutes on one of the hard four, runs out
of time, and never reaches 2 of the questions she could have solved. She leaves everything
she didn’t solve blank.

Student B skips each hard question when it comes up, solves all 11 she can, then goes back
and clicks a guess on the 4 hard ones.

How many more questions should Student B expect to get right?

<Solution>
**Student A:** she reaches only 9 of her 11 solvable questions, and her blanks score
nothing. Expected score: **9**.

**Student B:** all 11 solvable questions, plus 4 guesses at a 1-in-5 chance each:
4 × ⅕ = 0.8. Expected score: **11.8**.

Difference: about **3 questions on one section** — with identical knowledge. Across two
Quant and two Verbal sections, format habits alone can be worth several scaled points.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — the calculator">
Quant has an on-screen calculator, and it is slow: you click the buttons with a mouse.
Most GRE arithmetic is designed to be easier by hand or by estimation. Use the calculator
for ugly arithmetic you have already set up — never as a way to avoid thinking.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## Three rules that follow from the format

1. **Never leave a blank.** Before time runs out, click an answer on every question —
   even a pure guess.
2. **Bank time on the easy ones.** Every question counts the same within a section, so a
   quick right answer is worth exactly as much as a slow one.
3. **Move on after about two and a half minutes with no plan.** Mark it, guess, and come
   back if time remains. One stubborn question can cost you three easy ones.

You will practice these rules in the timed sections of the practice bank, which run at
exactly this pace.
</Concept>
