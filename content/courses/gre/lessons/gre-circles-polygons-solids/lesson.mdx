<Intuition>
This lesson has a lot of formulas, but they all come from a few simple ideas:

- **Everything about a circle comes from its radius.** Find r first, every time.
- **An arc or a sector is a fraction of the circle.** That fraction is the angle out of 360°.
- **Polygons break into triangles**, and a triangle's angles add to 180°.
- **A solid's volume is "base area × height"** for boxes and cylinders.

Once you see those, the formulas stop being things to memorize and start being things you can
rebuild.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. Circle basics

The **radius** r goes from the center to the edge. The **diameter** d goes all the way across through
the center, so **d = 2r**.

$$
C = 2\pi r \qquad\qquad A = \pi r^2
$$

- **Circumference** (C) is the distance around the circle.
- **Area** (A) is the space inside.

**The method: find r first.** Whatever you're given — diameter, circumference, or area — turn it into
the radius, then use the formula you need.

| You're given | Get r by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| diameter 10 | halve it | r = 5 |
| circumference 12π | 2πr = 12π | r = 6 |
| area 49π | πr² = 49π | r = 7 |

GRE answers are usually left **in terms of π** ("36π", not 113.1).
</Concept>

<Example title="Area to circumference">
A circle has an area of 49π. What is its circumference?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Find r.** πr² = 49π, so r² = 49 and r = 7.

**Step 2 — Use the circumference formula.** C = 2π(7) = **14π**.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Arcs and sectors: a slice of the circle

An **arc** is a piece of the circle's edge. A **sector** is a pizza-slice piece of its area. Both are
set by the **central angle** θ (the angle at the center).

The key idea: **the slice is the same fraction of the whole circle as θ is of 360°.** A 90° slice is
¼ of the circle; a 60° slice is ⅙.

$$
\text{arc length} = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r \qquad\qquad \text{sector area} = \frac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2
$$

It also works backward: if a sector is ⅕ of the circle's area, its central angle is ⅕ of 360°, which
is 72°.
</Concept>

<Example title="Arc and sector from one angle">
A circle has radius 9. A central angle of 80° cuts off an arc and a sector. Find the arc length and
the sector area.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — What fraction of the circle?** 80/360 = 2/9.

**Step 2 — Arc length.** The whole circumference is 2π(9) = 18π. Two-ninths of it: (2/9) × 18π = **4π**.

**Step 3 — Sector area.** The whole area is π(9²) = 81π. Two-ninths of it: (2/9) × 81π = **18π**.
</Solution>
</Example>

- **Square inside a circle** (corners on the circle): the square's **diagonal** is a **diameter**.
- **Circle inside a square** (touching all four sides): the circle's **diameter** equals the square's
  **side**.
- **Triangle with a diameter as one side** and its third corner on the circle: the angle at that
  third corner is **90°**. So you can use the Pythagorean theorem.
- A **tangent** line (touching the circle at one point) is **perpendicular** to the radius there.

**Shaded regions:** area of the shaded part = **big shape − small shape**.
</Concept>

<Example title="Square inside a circle">
A square is drawn inside a circle of radius 5 so that all four of its corners lie on the circle. What
is the area of the region inside the circle but outside the square?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Circle's area.** π(5²) = 25π.

**Step 2 — Find the shared length.** The square's diagonal is a diameter: 2 × 5 = 10.

**Step 3 — Square's area.** A square with diagonal 10 has side 10/√2 = 5√2, so its area is
(5√2)² = 50. (Shortcut: square area = diagonal² ÷ 2 = 100 ÷ 2.)

**Step 4 — Subtract.** Circle − square = **25π − 50** (about 28.5).
</Solution>
</Example>

## 4. Angles in polygons

Any polygon can be cut into triangles by drawing lines from one corner. A polygon with **n** sides
splits into **n − 2** triangles, and each triangle has 180°:

$$
\text{sum of the interior angles} = (n - 2) \times 180^\circ
$$

| Shape | Sides | Sum of angles |
|---|---|---|
| triangle | 3 | 180° |
| quadrilateral | 4 | 360° |
| pentagon | 5 | 540° |
| hexagon | 6 | 720° |

In a **regular** polygon (all sides and angles equal), each angle is the sum ÷ n.

**Faster route for regular polygons: the exterior angles.** The outside angles of any polygon always
add to **360°**, so each exterior angle of a regular polygon is 360° ÷ n. And each inside angle is
180° minus the outside angle.
</Concept>

<Example title="One angle of a regular hexagon">
What is the measure of each interior angle of a regular hexagon?

<Solution>
**Method 1 — Angle sum.** (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°. Split evenly among 6 angles: 720 ÷ 6 = **120°**.

**Method 2 — Exterior angle.** Each exterior angle is 360° ÷ 6 = 60°, so each interior angle is
180° − 60° = **120°**.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 5. Areas of four-sided shapes

| Shape | Area | Note |
|---|---|---|
| rectangle | length × width | diagonal = √(l² + w²) |
| square | side² | also diagonal² ÷ 2 |
| parallelogram | base × height | height is **perpendicular**, not the slanted side |
| trapezoid | ½ × (base₁ + base₂) × height | the bases are the two parallel sides |

**The height is always the straight-up-and-down distance** (at a right angle to the base). For a
parallelogram or trapezoid, that's usually **not** one of the sides — you often find it with a right
triangle.

The trapezoid formula makes sense: average the two parallel sides, then treat it like a rectangle.
</Concept>

<Example title="A trapezoid">
A trapezoid has parallel sides of length 8 and 12, and the distance between them is 5. What is its
area?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Average the parallel sides.** (8 + 12) ÷ 2 = 10.

**Step 2 — Multiply by the height.** 10 × 5 = **50**.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 6. Boxes, cubes, and cylinders

**Volume = area of the base × height** — for any box or cylinder.

| Solid | Volume | Surface area |
|---|---|---|
| box (l × w × h) | lwh | 2(lw + lh + wh) — the six faces |
| cube (side s) | s³ | 6s² — six equal square faces |
| cylinder (radius r, height h) | πr²h | 2πr² + 2πrh — two circles plus the wrapped side |

**Longest line inside a box** (corner to opposite corner): √(l² + w² + h²).

**Scaling:** if every length is multiplied by k, the volume is multiplied by **k³**. But if only
**one** measurement changes, just follow the formula — doubling a cylinder's radius multiplies its
volume by 4 (because r is squared), while doubling its height only doubles the volume.
</Concept>

<Example title="Longest line in a box">
What is the length of the longest straight segment that fits inside a box measuring 3 × 4 × 12?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Use the formula.** √(3² + 4² + 12²) = √(9 + 16 + 144) = √169 = **13**.

**Why it works:** it's two Pythagorean steps. The diagonal across the 3 × 4 floor is 5 (a 3-4-5
triangle). Then that floor diagonal and the height 12 make another right triangle: √(5² + 12²) = 13.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — radius or diameter?">
Questions often give the **diameter** while every formula uses the **radius**. Before you square
anything, write "r = ___" on your scratch paper.
</Gotcha>
