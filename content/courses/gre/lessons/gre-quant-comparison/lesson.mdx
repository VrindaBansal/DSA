<Intuition>
Quantitative comparison (QC) questions give you two quantities, A and B, and ask **which is bigger** —
or whether you **can't tell**. They make up about a third of the Quant section.

The big difference from regular math problems: **you don't need the exact values.** You only need to
know which side is bigger. So you can simplify, estimate, and — when there are variables — **try
numbers**. If one allowed number makes A bigger and another makes B bigger, you're done: the answer is
"cannot be determined."
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The four answers — and comparing numbers

Every QC question has the same four choices:

| Choice | Means |
|---|---|
| A | Quantity A is greater — **always**, for every allowed value |
| B | Quantity B is greater — **always** |
| C | The two are equal — **always** |
| D | It **depends** — the relationship can't be determined |

Two things follow from this:

1. **If there are no variables** (both quantities are plain numbers), **D is impossible**. Two fixed
   numbers always compare one way.
2. A, B, and C are claims about **every** case. **One counterexample kills them.**

**A routine for every QC question:**
1. Are there variables? If not, D is out.
2. Simplify both sides (next section).
3. If variables are left, test numbers (section 3).

**Comparing plain numbers:** don't grind out both values if you can see the structure. Look for ways to
rewrite one side to look like the other.
</Concept>

<Example title="Spot the structure">
| Quantity A | Quantity B |
|---|---|
| 17 × 19 | 18² |

<Solution>
**Step 1 — No variables,** so the answer is A, B, or C.

**Step 2 — Rewrite A to look like B.** 17 × 19 = (18 − 1)(18 + 1) = 18² − 1 (difference of squares).

**Step 3 — Compare.** 18² − 1 is less than 18². **Quantity B is greater.**

(Checking the arithmetic: 323 vs. 324.)
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Simplify both sides — safely

Treat the two quantities like the two sides of an equation you're allowed to change, **as long as the
change can't flip which one is bigger**:

| Move | Safe? |
|---|---|
| add or subtract the same thing from both | ✓ always |
| multiply or divide both by the same **positive** number | ✓ |
| square both | ✓ only if both are known to be **non-negative** |
| multiply or divide both by something that **could be negative or zero** | ✗ — it might flip the order |

**Example:** A = 3x + 7 and B = 3x + 5. Subtract 3x from both: A = 7, B = 5. A is bigger **no matter
what x is**.
</Concept>

<Example title="Expand, then cancel">
| Quantity A | Quantity B |
|---|---|
| (x + 1)² | x² + 2x |

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Expand A.** (x + 1)² = x² + 2x + 1.

**Step 2 — Subtract x² + 2x from both sides.** A becomes 1, B becomes 0.

**Step 3 — Compare.** 1 > 0, for every x. **Quantity A is greater.**
</Solution>
</Example>

## 3. Test numbers — from every zone

When a variable is left over, **plug in numbers** the question allows. The trick is to test
**different kinds** of numbers, not just 2, 3, and 5. Numbers behave differently in different "zones":
</Concept>

<TradeoffTable id="gre-number-zones" />

<Concept>
**How to run the tests:**
- Start with an easy number (like 2), then try the zones that behave strangely: **0, 1, a fraction, a
  negative, a negative fraction**.
- **As soon as two tests disagree, stop — the answer is D.**
- If all your tests agree, look for the **reason** it always works. That's what confirms the answer
  isn't D.
</Concept>

<Example title="Two tests disagree">
x ≠ 0

| Quantity A | Quantity B |
|---|---|
| x² | x³ |

<Solution>
**Test 1 — x = 2.** A = 4, B = 8 → **B** is bigger.

**Test 2 — x = −1** (a negative). A = 1, B = −1 → **A** is bigger.

**Two allowed values disagree, so the answer is D.** You can stop after the second test.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Example title="Tests agree — find the reason">
0 < x < 1

| Quantity A | Quantity B |
|---|---|
| x | √x |

<Solution>
**Test 1 — x = ¼.** A = 0.25, B = 0.5 → B bigger.

**Test 2 — near the edge, x = 0.81.** A = 0.81, B = 0.9 → B bigger.

**The reason:** for a number between 0 and 1, taking the square root makes it **bigger** (the opposite
of squaring). So B wins every time. **Quantity B is greater.**
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Hidden cases

Many QC traps hide a second possibility the question never mentions:

- **Even powers hide the sign.** x² = 16 means x = 4 **or** x = −4.
- **Products hide the signs.** If ab = 12, a and b could both be **negative**.
- **Figures aren't drawn to scale.** Unless the question says so, don't assume a right angle, equal
  sides, or parallel lines — even if the picture looks that way.

Before you pick A, B, or C, ask: **"Is there another case I haven't tried?"**
</Concept>

<Gotcha title="Trap — y² = 49 doesn't mean y = 7">
It's tempting to answer C when you're told y² = 49 and asked to compare y with 7. But y could be −7.
If the question doesn't say y is positive, keep both roots.
</Gotcha>

<Example title="A triangle that isn't known to be right">
In triangle ABC, AB = 6 and BC = 8.

| Quantity A | Quantity B |
|---|---|
| AC | 10 |

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Don't assume a right angle.** If angle B were 90°, AC would be 10 (a 6-8-10 triangle) — but
nothing says it is.

**Step 2 — What can AC be?** By the triangle rule, AC is between 8 − 6 = 2 and 8 + 6 = 14.

**Step 3 — Compare.** AC could be 5 (B bigger), 10 (equal), or 12 (A bigger). **The answer is D.**
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 5. Word-problem comparisons

A few classic setups show up again and again:

- **Percent vs. percentage points.** A rate going from 20% to 25% rose by **5 percentage points** —
  but by **25 percent** (5 ÷ 20).
- **Averages.** If the average of x and y is 20, then x + y = 40. So if x is **above** 20, y must be
  **below** 20 by the same amount.
- **Look-alikes that are equal.** 30% of 80 and 80% of 30 are both 24. In general, **x% of y always
  equals y% of x.**
</Concept>

<Example title="Swap the percents">
| Quantity A | Quantity B |
|---|---|
| 45% of 60 | 60% of 45 |

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Write both as multiplication.** A = 0.45 × 60. B = 0.60 × 45.

**Step 2 — See the pattern.** Both are 45 × 60 ÷ 100 — the same numbers multiplied in a different order.

**Answer: the quantities are equal** (both 27).
</Solution>
</Example>

## Recap

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| no variables | D is impossible; rewrite one side to look like the other |
| same terms on both sides | cancel them (add/subtract, or × ÷ by a positive) |
| variables left | test 2, then 0, 1, ½, −1, −½; two different results → D |
| tests all agree | find the reason before choosing A, B, or C |
| x² = k, ab = k, a figure | look for the hidden case (negatives, not to scale) |
| percent changes | percentage points ≠ percent; x% of y = y% of x |
</Concept>
