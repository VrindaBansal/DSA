<Intuition>
A text completion is a sentence (or a few) with a word missing and five choices. It feels like a
vocabulary test, but it's really a **logic** test: every sentence is written so that **exactly one**
answer can be defended with evidence from the sentence itself.

That evidence has two parts:
- a **clue** — words elsewhere in the sentence that describe what the blank must mean, and
- often a **signal** — a word like *although* or *because* that tells you whether the blank **agrees**
  with the clue or **contrasts** with it.

So don't ask "which word sounds good?" Ask "what does the sentence **prove** the blank means?" Words
that just sound impressive are the test-writer's favorite trap.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The method: cover, clue, predict, match

1. **Cover the choices.** Read only the sentence.
2. **Find the clue** — the part of the sentence that describes the missing idea.
3. **Find the signal** (if there is one) — does the blank go the **same way** as the clue, or the
   **opposite** way?
4. **Predict** the blank in your own simple words: "careful," "not interested," "made it worse." Plain
   words are fine — you're predicting a meaning, not a vocabulary word.
5. **Match.** Now look at the choices and pick the one closest to your prediction.

Predicting **before** you look is the key step. If you look at the choices first, the fancy wrong
answers will start to sound right.
</Concept>

<Example title="Cover, clue, predict, match">
"The documentary was praised for its ___, presenting the evidence on both sides of the controversy
without tipping the scales toward either."

*zeal · impartiality · brevity · ambiguity · whimsy*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Cover the choices** and read the sentence.

**Step 2 — Clue.** "presenting the evidence on both sides … without tipping the scales toward either."

**Step 3 — Signal.** The comma phrase **explains** what it was praised for — same direction as the clue.

**Step 4 — Predict.** Fairness, balance.

**Step 5 — Match.** **Impartiality** (treating all sides equally) ✓.

Why the others fail: *zeal* (enthusiasm) — no clue for it. *Brevity* — nothing about length.
*Ambiguity* (unclear meaning) — tempting, since "not taking a side" can feel vague, but the sentence
praises clear balance. *Whimsy* (playfulness) — off-topic.
</Solution>
</Example>

## 2. Signal words: which way does the blank point?

The same clue can call for opposite words, depending on the signal:

- "The critic was usually harsh, **and** this review was no exception: it was ___." → **harsh**
- "The critic was usually harsh, **but** this review was surprisingly ___." → **kind**

There are three kinds of signals:
- **Same direction** (*and, because, so, in fact, similarly*) — the blank agrees with the clue.
- **Opposite direction** (*but, although, yet, despite, however, far from*) — the blank contrasts with
  the clue.
- **Explanation** (a **colon**, a **semicolon**, or **dashes**) — what comes after explains or restates
  the blank. These are the most helpful: the clue practically defines the blank.
</Concept>

<TradeoffTable id="gre-signal-words" />

<Example title="A contrast signal">
"Although the scientist's early papers were ___, full of speculation that few colleagues took seriously,
her later work was rigorous and widely cited."

*meticulous · conjectural · influential · lucid · canonical*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Clue.** "full of speculation that few colleagues took seriously." This practically defines
the blank.

**Step 2 — Signal.** "Although … her later work was rigorous" — the early papers **contrast** with the
rigorous later work.

**Step 3 — Predict.** Speculative, unproven.

**Step 4 — Match.** **Conjectural** (based on guesses rather than evidence) ✓.

**The trap:** *meticulous* (very careful) matches "rigorous" — but that describes the **later** work, the
other side of the contrast.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. When you don't know a word

You won't know every word in every question. Don't guess at random — **eliminate**:

1. Make your prediction as usual.
2. **Cross off every word you know** that doesn't match it. (Charge helps: if the prediction is
   negative, every positive word goes.)
3. If one unknown word is left, it's very likely the answer. Test-writers rarely make an obscure word a
   *wrong* answer when a familiar word would do the job.
4. If you can, confirm with roots (*in-* = not, *mal-* = bad, *bene-* = good).
</Concept>

<Example title="Elimination">
"The negotiator's ___ manner — calm, patient, never raising her voice — helped settle even the angriest
disputes."

*phlegmatic · irascible · volatile · belligerent · histrionic*

Suppose you don't know *phlegmatic*.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Predict.** The dashes define the blank: calm, patient, unemotional.

**Step 2 — Cross off what you know.**
- *Irascible* — easily angered ✗
- *Volatile* — likely to explode ✗
- *Belligerent* — hostile, ready to fight ✗
- *Histrionic* — overly dramatic ✗

**Step 3 — One word left: phlegmatic.** It means calm and hard to excite ✓. Every word you knew was the
opposite of the clue, so the unknown word had to be the answer.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Fits the topic ≠ fits the logic

Wrong answers are often words that **go with the subject** of the sentence. In a sentence about
science, *empirical* and *experimental* feel right; in one about art, *aesthetic* and *abstract*. But a
word isn't right because it belongs to the topic. It's right because the **clue demands its meaning**.

**The fix:** always predict from the clue first. Then a topic word that doesn't match your prediction
is easy to reject.
</Concept>

<Example title="The topic trap">
"Although the report was packed with charts and technical terms, its central claim was ___, resting
on a single interview with one unnamed source."

*quantitative · unsubstantiated · empirical · statistical · rigorous*

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Clue.** "resting on a single interview with one unnamed source."

**Step 2 — Signal.** "Although … packed with charts" — the claim **contrasts** with how scientific the
report looks.

**Step 3 — Predict.** Poorly supported.

**Step 4 — Match.** **Unsubstantiated** (not supported by evidence) ✓.

*Quantitative, empirical, statistical,* and *rigorous* all fit the **topic** (charts, technical terms)
— which is exactly the side of the contrast the blank is against.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — the sophisticated-sounding word">
If you catch yourself choosing a word because it "sounds smart" or "sounds like the topic," stop and
go back to your prediction. The right answer matches the clue; it doesn't have to be the hardest word.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## Recap

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| cover | read the sentence without the choices |
| clue | find the words that describe the blank |
| signal | same direction, opposite, or explanation (colon, dash) |
| predict | say the blank in your own plain words |
| match | pick the choice closest to the prediction |
| unknown word | eliminate every known word that doesn't fit |
| topic trap | a word that fits the subject isn't enough — it must fit the clue |
</Concept>
