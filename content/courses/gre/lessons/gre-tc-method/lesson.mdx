<Intuition>
Every text completion sentence is written so that **exactly one** answer is defensible.
That means the sentence itself contains the evidence — a **clue** that tells you what the
blank must mean, and often a **signal word** that tells you whether the blank agrees with
the clue or contrasts with it. Your job is not to “see which word sounds good.” It is to
find the evidence, predict the meaning, and only then look for the word that matches.
Words that merely sound sophisticated are the test-writer’s favorite trap.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## The method: cover, clue, predict, match

1. **Cover the choices.** Read only the sentence.
2. **Find the clue** — the part of the sentence that describes the blank. It is usually a
   phrase elsewhere in the sentence that restates or contrasts with the missing idea.
3. **Find the signal** — a word like *although*, *because*, *and*, or a colon that tells you
   which way the blank points relative to the clue.
4. **Predict** a simple word or phrase for the blank. Your own words are fine: “stubborn,”
   “not interested,” “made it worse.”
5. **Match**: now look at the choices and pick the one closest to your prediction. If two
   seem close, check each back against the clue.
</Concept>

<Example title="Cover, clue, predict, match">
The documentary was praised for its ___, presenting the evidence on both sides of the
controversy without tipping the scales toward either.

*zeal · impartiality · brevity · ambiguity · whimsy*

<Solution>
**Clue:** “presenting the evidence on both sides … without tipping the scales.”
**Signal:** the comma phrase *explains* what it was praised for — same direction.
**Prediction:** fairness, balance.

**Match:** *impartiality* (treating all sides equally). ✓

The others: *zeal* (enthusiasm) — no clue for it; *brevity* — nothing about length;
*ambiguity* (unclear meaning) — tempting because “not taking a side” can feel vague, but
the sentence praises clear balance, not unclear meaning; *whimsy* — playfulness, off-topic.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## Signal words point the direction

The same clue can demand opposite words depending on the signal:

- “The critic was usually harsh, **and** this review was no exception: it was ___.” → harsh
- “The critic was usually harsh, **but** this review was surprisingly ___.” → kind
</Concept>

<Example title="A contrast signal">
Although the scientist’s early papers were ___, full of speculation that few colleagues
took seriously, her later work was rigorous and widely cited.

*meticulous · conjectural · influential · lucid · canonical*

<Solution>
**Clue:** “full of speculation that few colleagues took seriously.” That clue actually
defines the blank directly. **Signal:** “although … her later work was rigorous” — the
blank contrasts with *rigorous*.

Prediction: speculative, unproven. **Match:** *conjectural*. ✓

The trap is *meticulous*: it matches “rigorous,” which describes the **later** work, the
opposite side of the contrast.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Check id="gre-tc1-signal" />

<Gotcha title="Trap — the word that fits the topic but not the logic">
Wrong answers are often words that “go with” the sentence’s subject. In a sentence about
science, *empirical* and *rigorous* feel right; in one about art, *aesthetic*. Don’t pick
a word because it belongs to the topic. Pick it because the clue demands its meaning.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## When you don’t know a word

Eliminate what you do know first. If your prediction is negative, cross off every positive
word you recognize. If only one unknown word is left, it is usually the answer — test-writers
rarely make an obscure word a wrong answer when a known word would do. Then check any roots
(*in-* not, *mal-* bad, *bene-* good) for confirmation.
</Concept>
