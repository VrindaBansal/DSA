<Intuition>
Statistics on the GRE asks two kinds of questions about a list of numbers:

1. **Where is the middle?** — the mean (average) and the median.
2. **How spread out are the numbers?** — the range, the interquartile range, and the standard deviation.

You'll almost never calculate a standard deviation by hand. You'll **compare** spreads, which is much
easier. And the single best trick for averages: **switch to sums.** Sums can be added and subtracted;
averages can't.
</Intuition>

<Concept>
## 1. The mean — think in sums

The **mean** (average) is the sum divided by how many numbers there are. Flip that around and you get
the most useful equation in this lesson:

$$
\text{sum} = \text{average} \times \text{count}
$$

Whenever numbers are **added to or removed from** a list, turn every average into a sum, do the adding
or subtracting with the sums, then turn back into an average if needed.
</Concept>

<Example title="Adding a number to a list">
The average of 5 numbers is 12. When a sixth number is added, the average becomes 14. What is the sixth
number?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Old sum.** 5 × 12 = 60.

**Step 2 — New sum.** 6 × 14 = 84.

**Step 3 — The difference is the new number.** 84 − 60 = **24**.

**Sanity check:** the new number had to be above 14 to pull the average up, and it had to "pay" 2
extra points to each of the 5 old numbers: 14 + 5 × 2 = 24 ✓.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 2. Weighted averages — combining groups

When you combine two groups of **different sizes**, you **can't** just average the two averages. The
bigger group has more say. Use sums again:

$$
\text{combined average} = \frac{\text{total of group 1} + \text{total of group 2}}{\text{total count}}
$$

**The picture:** the combined average always lands **between** the two group averages, **closer to
the bigger group's**. If the groups are the same size, it's exactly halfway.
</Concept>

<Example title="Two classes">
One class of 20 students averages 80 on a test, and another class of 30 students averages 90. What is
the average for all 50 students?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Totals.** Class 1: 20 × 80 = 1,600. Class 2: 30 × 90 = 2,700.

**Step 2 — Combine.** (1,600 + 2,700) ÷ 50 = 4,300 ÷ 50 = **86**.

**Check the picture:** 86 is between 80 and 90, and closer to 90 — the bigger class's average ✓. Just
averaging 80 and 90 would give 85, which is wrong.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 3. Median, mode, and range

- **Median** — the middle number **after sorting**. With an **even** count, there are two middle
  numbers; the median is their average.
- **Mode** — the number that appears **most often**.
- **Range** — **biggest minus smallest**.

For 3, 9, 4, 9, 20 → sorted 3, 4, 9, 9, 20: median 9, mode 9, range 20 − 3 = 17.

**Outliers** (numbers far from the rest) pull the **mean** strongly toward them and stretch the
**range**, but the **median** barely moves — it only cares about the order of the numbers, not how
extreme the end ones are.
</Concept>

<Example title="Median with an even count">
What is the median of 8, 15, 3, 12, 20, 6?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Sort.** 3, 6, 8, 12, 15, 20.

**Step 2 — Find the middle.** Six numbers, so the middle two are the 3rd and 4th: 8 and 12.

**Step 3 — Average them.** (8 + 12) ÷ 2 = **10**.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 4. Quartiles and the interquartile range

The median cuts the sorted data in half. **Quartiles** cut it into **four equal parts**:

- **Q1** (first quartile) — the median of the **lower half**.
- **Q2** — the median itself.
- **Q3** (third quartile) — the median of the **upper half**.

So about 25% of the data is below Q1, 50% is below the median, and 75% is below Q3.

The **interquartile range** (IQR) is **Q3 − Q1**: how spread out the **middle half** of the data is.
Unlike the range, it ignores extreme values at the ends.

A **percentile** says what percent of the data is at or below a value. Q1 is about the 25th
percentile, the median the 50th, and Q3 the 75th.
</Concept>

<Example title="Finding the IQR">
Find the interquartile range of 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 15.

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Split in half.** Eight numbers (already sorted): lower half 1, 3, 4, 6; upper half 8, 9, 12, 15.

**Step 2 — Median of each half.** Q1 = (3 + 4) ÷ 2 = 3.5. Q3 = (9 + 12) ÷ 2 = 10.5.

**Step 3 — Subtract.** IQR = 10.5 − 3.5 = **7**.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Concept>
## 5. Standard deviation — how spread out?

The **standard deviation** (SD) measures how far the numbers **typically are from their mean**.
Numbers bunched close together → small SD. Numbers spread far apart → big SD. You'll almost never need
the formula; you need these facts:

- If all the numbers are the **same**, the SD is **0**.
- **Adding** the same amount to every number **doesn't change** the SD. (Everything slides over
  together; the gaps stay the same.)
- **Multiplying** every number by k multiplies the SD by k (ignoring any minus sign).
- **Size doesn't matter, spacing does.** 1, 2, 3 and 101, 102, 103 have the **same** SD. And 2, 4, 6
  has a bigger SD than 1, 2, 3 — its gaps are twice as wide.

**To compare SDs:** look at how far the numbers sit from their own middle.
</Concept>

<Example title="Shift and stretch">
A list of numbers has mean 20 and SD 3. Every number is doubled, and then 5 is added to each. What are
the new mean and SD?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Mean.** The mean follows every operation: 20 × 2 + 5 = **45**.

**Step 2 — SD.** Doubling doubles the spread: 3 × 2 = 6. Adding 5 slides everything over without
changing the gaps, so the SD stays **6**.
</Solution>
</Example>

Adding up the pieces gives the famous rule:
- about **68%** of the data is within 1 SD of the mean (34 + 34),
- about **95%** is within 2 SDs (13.5 + 34 + 34 + 13.5),
- about **99.7%** is within 3 SDs.

**The method:** turn each value into "how many SDs from the mean," then add up the pieces of the
picture. Example: with mean 60 and SD 10, a value of 70 is +1 SD, so about 50% + 34% = 84% of the data
is below 70, and 16% is above it.
</Concept>

<Example title="Between two values">
Test scores are normally distributed with mean 500 and SD 100. About what percent of scores are between
400 and 700?

<Solution>
**Step 1 — Convert to SDs.** 400 is 100 below the mean = −1 SD. 700 is 200 above = +2 SD.

**Step 2 — Add the pieces from −1 SD to +2 SD.** 34% (−1 to mean) + 34% (mean to +1) + 13.5% (+1 to +2).

**Step 3 — Total.** 34 + 34 + 13.5 = **81.5%**.
</Solution>
</Example>

<Gotcha title="Trap — averaging the averages">
The average of two group averages equals the overall average **only if the groups are the same size**.
Otherwise, go through the totals.
</Gotcha>

<Concept>
## Recap

| Idea | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| mean | sum = average × count | avg 12 of 5 → sum 60 |
| weighted average | add the totals, divide by total count; lands nearer the bigger group | 20 at 80 + 30 at 90 → 86 |
| median | sort; middle value, or average of the two middle | 3, 6, 8, 12, 15, 20 → 10 |
| outlier | moves the mean and range a lot, the median a little | add 100 to 3, 5, 9: median 5 → 7, mean ≈ 5.7 → 29.25 |
| quartiles / IQR | medians of the lower and upper halves; IQR = Q3 − Q1 | 1 … 15 above → IQR 7 |
| SD | spacing, not size; + k: no change; × k: SD × k | 1, 2, 3 and 101, 102, 103: same SD |
| normal curve | 34 / 13.5 / 2.5 on each side | 68% within 1 SD, 95% within 2 |
</Concept>
